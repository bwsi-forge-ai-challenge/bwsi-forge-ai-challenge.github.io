Derivatives and Instantaneous Rate of Change

In the previous lesson, we explored the idea of a tangent line. We saw that a tangent line touches a curve at a single point and tells us how the function is changing at that exact location.

The slope of the tangent line actually has its own name: the derivative.

A derivative measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function. In other words, it tells us how quickly the output of a function is changing at a specific value of \(x\).

1. From Tangent Lines to Derivatives

Recall that in the previous lesson, moving the tangent point changed the slope of the tangent line.

These slopes describe how the function behaves locally:

  • Positive slopes mean the function is increasing.
  • Negative slopes mean the function is decreasing.
  • A slope of zero indicates a horizontal tangent line.

If we continuously track the derivative at each point of a function \(f(x)\), we can define a new function \(f'(x)\), referred to as the derivative of \(f(x)\).

2. Derivative Notation

Different textbooks and fields use different symbols for derivatives. The two most common are:

Name Notation Read As
Lagrange Notation \(f'(x)\) "f prime of x"
Leibniz Notation \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) "dy dx"

Although these symbols look different, they describe the same quantity: the slope of the tangent line.

3. Derivatives as rates of change

Derivatives are not just geometric slopes. They also represent real-world rates of change.

Velocity

If position changes over time, the derivative of position gives velocity:

v(t) = ds/dt
  • Position is stable → zero velocity
  • Moving forwards → positive velocity
  • Moving backwards → negative velocity

Acceleration

The derivative of velocity is acceleration:

a(t) = dv/dt
  • Constant speed → zero acceleration
  • Speeding up → direction of acceleration matches velocity
  • Slowing down → direction of acceleration is the opposite of velocity

5. The Chain Rule

Let's take a closer look at the final row of the table above. To differentiate composite functions, we must utilize a formula called the Chain Rule.

The Chain Rule says:

\[ \frac{d}{dx} [f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) \]
Another common way of writing the chain rule is in Leibniz notation:
\[ \frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{dz}{dy} \times \frac{dy}{dx} \]
You may notice that we can keep applying the chain rule in this fashion to obtain the following:
\[ \frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{dz}{dy_0} \times \frac{dy_0}{dy_1} \times \frac{dy_1}{dy_2} \times \cdots \times \frac{dy_n}{dx} \]

Let's take a look at an example:

\( h(x)=(3x^2+1)^5 \)

We can define our composite functions \(f(z)=z^5\) and \(g(z)=3z^2+1\).

As such, \(f'(z)=5z^4\) and \(g'(z)=6z\). Then

\(h'(x)= f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)=5(3x+1)^4\cdot 6x = 30x(3x+1)^4 \)

6. Maxima and Minima

Derivatives not only tell us how a function is changing, but they also help us find where a function reaches its highest or lowest points within a certain range. These points are called local maxima and local minima. Across the entire function, the highest and lowest points are called the absolute maxima and absolute minima, respectively. A function can have several local maxima and minima but only up to one absolute maximum and absolute minimum.

A key idea is that at a maximum or minimum point, the tangent line is horizontal. This means:

\[ f'(x)=0 \]

7. The Second Derivative

The second derivative is the derivative of the derivative. It is written as:

\[ f''(x) \]
or
\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \]

While the first derivative tells us the slope of a function, the second derivative tells us how that slope is changing. This is often described as the concavity of the graph.

  • If \(f''(x) > 0\), the graph is concave up (shaped like a cup).
  • If \(f''(x) < 0\), the graph is concave down (shaped like a cap).

We call a point where concavity changes (which requires \(f''(x) = 0\)) an inflection point. Let's see how we can use these terms to label the previous function \(f(x)=x^3-x\).

8. Connecting to Maxima and Minima

The second derivative can also help classify critical points:

  • If \(f'(x)=0\) and \(f''(x) > 0\), then \(x\) is a local minimum.
  • If \(f'(x)=0\) and \(f''(x) < 0\), then \(x\) is a local maximum.

9. Extending to Higher-Order Derivatives

We can continue differentiating a function multiple times:

\[ f(x),\quad f'(x),\quad f''(x),\quad f'''(x),\quad \dots,\quad f^{(n)}(x) \]

These are called higher-order derivatives. In Leibniz notation, we express these as

\[ y,\frac{dy}{dx},\frac{d^2y}{dx^2},\quad \frac{d^3y}{dx^3},\quad \dots,\quad \frac{d^ny}{dx^n} \]

They are useful in physics, engineering, and advanced mathematics for describing motion, acceleration, and more complex change. In general, each additional derivative gives us a deeper layer of information about how a function behaves.
