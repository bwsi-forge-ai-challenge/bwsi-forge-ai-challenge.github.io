Vectors

A concise review of vector fundamentals for machine learning

This chapter is a condensed reference. If you need a deeper refresher, 3Blue1Brown's Essence of Linear Algebra is an excellent visual treatment of everything covered here.

1. What is a vector?

A vector is an ordered finite list of numbers. We write it as a column and denote it with a bold letter or arrow:

\[\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n\]

The number of entries is its dimension. A vector in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) has \(n\) entries, each a real number. Geometrically, a vector is an arrow from the origin to a point. Each entry corresponds to movement along one independent axis.

2. Vector operations

Transpose

The transpose flips a column vector into a row vector (and vice versa). No numbers change:

\[\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 7 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\]

Addition

Element-wise, defined only for vectors of the same size:

\[\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}\]

Properties: commutative, associative, identity \(\vec{x} + \vec{0} = \vec{x}\), inverse \(\vec{x} - \vec{x} = \vec{0}\).

Scalar multiplication

Multiply every entry by the scalar \(\alpha\):

\[\alpha \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_1 \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_n \end{bmatrix}\]

Geometrically: stretches, shrinks, or flips the arrow. Direction stays the same (or reverses for negative \(\alpha\)).

Linear combinations

Scale vectors and add them. Given vectors \(\vec{x}_1, \ldots, \vec{x}_k\) and scalars \(\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_k\):

\[\sum_{i=1}^{k} \beta_i \vec{x}_i = \beta_1 \vec{x}_1 + \cdots + \beta_k \vec{x}_k\]

Dot product

Multiply corresponding entries and sum into a single scalar:

\[\vec{x} \cdot \vec{y} = \vec{x}^T \vec{y} = x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2 + \cdots + x_n y_n\]

Example:

\[\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -5 \end{bmatrix}^T \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = (3)(2) + (-5)(4) = -14\]

The dot product measures alignment between two vectors. Positive means roughly same direction, negative means roughly opposite, zero means perpendicular.

3. Vector norms and normalization

A norm measures the length (magnitude) of a vector. The L2 (Euclidean) norm:

\[\|\vec{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2}\]

Properties: always \(\geq 0\) (zero only for \(\vec{0}\)), \(\|\alpha \vec{x}\| = |\alpha| \|\vec{x}\|\), triangle inequality \(\|\vec{x} + \vec{y}\| \leq \|\vec{x}\| + \|\vec{y}\|\).

Normalization produces a unit vector (length 1) preserving only direction:

\[\hat{x} = \frac{\vec{x}}{\|\vec{x}\|}\]

Distance between two vectors: \(\|\vec{a} - \vec{b}\|\).

4. Special vectors

Zero vector (\(\vec{0}\)): all entries 0. Identity element for addition.

Standard basis vectors (\(\vec{e}_i\)): entry \(i\) is 1, all others 0. Every vector is a linear combination of basis vectors:

\[\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -3 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = 5\vec{e}_1 - 3\vec{e}_2 + 7\vec{e}_3\]

Sparse vectors: most entries are zero.

5. Linear independence

Vectors \(\vec{x}_1, \ldots, \vec{x}_k\) are linearly independent if the only solution to:

\[\vec{0} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \beta_i \vec{x}_i\]

is \(\beta_1 = \beta_2 = \cdots = \beta_k = 0\). If any non-trivial combination produces \(\vec{0}\), the set is linearly dependent — at least one vector is redundant (expressible as a combination of the others).
