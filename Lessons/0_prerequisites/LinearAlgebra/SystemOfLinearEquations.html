Systems of Linear Equations

1. What is a system of linear equations?

A system of linear equations is a collection of one or more linear equations involving the same set of variables. For example, a system of two equations with two unknowns (\(x_1\) and \(x_2\)) looks like this:

\[3x_1 + 2x_2 = 7\] \[-x_1 + 4x_2 = 7\]

To solve the system means to find values for \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) that make both equations true at the same time. In this case, the unique solution is \(x_1 = 1\) and \(x_2 = 2\).

2. The Matrix Form: \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\)

Any linear system can be rewritten compactly using the matrix-vector multiplication layouts learned in the previous sections. We organize the system into three parts:

  • A coefficient matrix (\(A\)) holding the scalar weights
  • A variable vector (\(\vec{x}\)) holding the unknowns
  • A constant vector (\(\vec{b}\)) holding the outputs

Using our initial example from Section 1, the matrix equation is structured as:

\[\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}\]

Performing row-vector multiplication returns you exactly back to the scalar equations: \((3 \cdot x_1 + 2 \cdot x_2 = 7)\) and \((-1 \cdot x_1 + 4 \cdot x_2 = 7)\).

3. Solving with Matrix Inverses

If the coefficient matrix \(A\) is a square matrix and possesses an inverse (\(A^{-1}\)), we can algebraically solve the system \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b}\) by multiplying both sides by the inverse from the left side:

\[A^{-1}A\vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}\] \[I\vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}\] \[\vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{b}\]

Let's look at a concrete computational example:

\[\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}\]

Given that the inverse of the matrix \(A\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), we calculate the variable values directly:

\[\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (3 \cdot 4) + (-1 \cdot 11) \\ (-5 \cdot 4) + (2 \cdot 11) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 - 11 \\ -20 + 22 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\]

4. Number of Solutions

A linear system will always fall into one of exactly three structural categories:

  1. Exactly one unique solution: The equations represent lines or planes that intersect at a single distinct point. This occurs when \(A\) is fully invertible.
  2. Infinitely many solutions: The equations represent lines or planes that completely overlap. There are infinite valid coordinate combinations.
  3. No solution: The equations represent parallel paths that never cross. No possible coordinate configuration can satisfy all parameters simultaneously.
