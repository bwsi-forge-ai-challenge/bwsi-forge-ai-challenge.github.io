Linear Regression and Gradient Descent

Lesson 1 outlined the generalized optimization framework: data, model, loss, and iteration. This lesson implements that framework using its simplest variant: fitting a straight line to empirical data using gradient descent.

1. The line as a model

Linear regression estimates a continuous target value by calculating a weighted sum of the input features plus a constant offset. For a single independent variable x, the hypothesis takes the standard form:

ŷ = mx + b

  • m — slope: The rate of change in ŷ relative to a one-unit increase in x.
  • b — bias: The baseline prediction when the input x equals zero, also known as the y-intercept.
  • ŷ — The scalar output estimated by the model for a given value of x.

House prices

Consider a sample dataset where a 1,400 sq ft property sells for $310,000 and a 2,100 sq ft property sells for $420,000. While physical dimensions alone do not definitively dictate real estate value—given unaccounted variables like location, age, and renovations—a linear fit provides a reliable baseline approximation. An optimized fit might yield the equation price ≈ 95 × sqft + 180,000, indicating that each additional square foot scales the valuation by $95 on average.

Temperature over days

Tracking daily high temperatures over a two-week period in early spring reveals an upward trajectory, despite minor daily fluctuations: day 1 records 52°F, day 7 reaches 58°F, and day 14 registers 64°F. A linear equation with a slope of +0.9°F per day and an intercept of 51°F effectively models this seasonal shift without requiring every empirical data point to intersect the line directly.

Study hours and test scores

An evaluation of twelve students shows a positive correlation between preparation time and performance. For instance, an individual who studied for 3 hours scores a 62, while another who prepared for 9 hours scores an 88. Although variance exists—such as two students dedicating 6 hours yet finishing 15 points apart—the regression line isolates the average trend from the expected statistical noise.

2. Mean Squared Error

To optimize a model, we require a quantitative objective function that evaluates global prediction error. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) metric calculates the arithmetic mean of the squared differences between observed values and predictions across n observations:

MSE = (1/n) Σᵢ (yᵢ - (mxᵢ + b))²

Squaring the residuals serves two key mathematical functions: it eliminates negative signs so that errors on either side of the line accumulate positively, and it applies a progressive penalty to larger variances. A residual of 10 increases the error metric by 100, whereas a residual of 2 contributes only 4.

Geometrically, each data point's error corresponds to a vertical distance to the regression line. The square projected from this vertical segment represents that point's squared residual; because large variances produce exponentially larger areas, the optimization algorithm prioritizes minimizing these major deviations.

Why outliers pull the line

If a dataset contains nine properties clustered tightly along a modest valuation trend and one anomalous estate valued at $900,000 where the neighborhood baseline suggests $620,000, the resulting $280,000 residual squares to 78,400,000,000. This single value can outscale the combined squared errors of the other nine observations. Consequently, gradient descent alters parameters m and b to reduce this massive individual error term, pulling the regression line toward the outlier and away from the dense cluster. While MSE is inherently sensitive to such anomalies, it remains the standard objective function because its surface is smooth and continuously differentiable.

3. Gradient descent

Gradient descent iteratively modifies parameters m and b to minimize the cost surface. The computed gradient ∇L establishes the vector of steepest ascent across the loss landscape; evaluating the negative gradient yields the direction of steepest descent. Conceptually, this mirrors navigating a terrain under zero visibility: by evaluating the local slope beneath your feet, you can take a controlled step downward, re-evaluate the local orientation, and repeat the process until the gradient approaches zero.

For a single training instance with prediction ŷ = mx + b and loss L = (y − ŷ)², applying the partial differentiation chain rule yields:

∂L/∂m = −2x(y − mx − b)
∂L/∂b = −2(y − mx − b)

Averaging these partial derivatives over all n instances and applying a scalar learning rate α establishes the parameter update steps:

m ← m − α (∂MSE/∂m)
b ← b − α (∂MSE/∂b)

When predictions fall short of target values (y > ŷ), the resulting update shifts parameters m and b upward. The hyperparameter α regulates the step magnitude: excessively conservative learning rates slow convergence, while excessively high learning rates cause the parameters to overshoot the minimum, resulting in systemic oscillation or divergence.
