How Do LLMs Actually Learn?

ChatGPT feels like magic the first time you use it. But underneath, large language models (LLMs) are trained with the same core idea you already saw earlier: define a loss function, measure how wrong the model is, and use gradient descent to adjust parameters and reduce that loss.

The difference is scale. Instead of fitting a line to a few hundred housing prices, an LLM reads enormous amounts of text and adjusts billions of weights so it gets better at one deceptively simple task: predict the next token.

This lesson starts with an overview of the full training pipeline, then walks through each stage: where training data comes from, what pretraining and fine-tuning actually do, why RLHF exists, and why "bigger model + more data" keeps showing up in the news.

1. The Big Picture: From Raw Text to Assistant

Before looking at any one stage, it helps to see the whole pipeline. A chatbot like ChatGPT is not trained in a single step. It goes through several stages, and each one starts from the model the previous stage produced:

StageWhat goes inWhat the model learnsWhat comes out
Data collectionWeb pages, books, codeNothing yet. This stage gathers, filters, and cleans the text.A training dataset of trillions of tokens
PretrainingThat datasetLanguage, facts, and patterns, by predicting the next tokenA base model that continues text
Fine-tuningThousands to millions of example conversationsHow to follow instructions and answer like an assistantAn instruction-following model
RLHFHuman rankings of the model's answersWhich of several good answers people preferThe assistant that gets deployed

Two ideas tie the stages together. First, every stage uses the same basic machinery: a loss function and gradient descent, which is reviewed next. What changes from stage to stage is the data and what the loss rewards. Second, the stages are very unequal in cost. Pretraining uses almost all of the data, time, and money, while the later stages are comparatively quick adjustments. The scaling laws later in this lesson explain why pretraining keeps getting bigger.

2. The Simple Idea at the Core

Remember training a neural network in the prerequisites? You had:

  • A model with parameters (weights)
  • A loss function that scores how bad the predictions are
  • Gradient descent to nudge weights in the direction that lowers the loss

LLM training uses the exact same loop. The task is just framed as language modeling:

Next-token prediction: Given some text so far, predict the token that comes next. Repeat across trillions of examples.
Given tokens \(x_1, x_2, x_{t - 1}\), the model outputs a probability for every possible next token.
Loss = how far those probabilities are from the true next token (cross-entropy).
Update weights with gradient descent. Repeat.

Example: for the phrase "The cat sat on the" the model might assign high probability to mat, moderate probability to floor, and low probability to airplane. When it picks the wrong token, the loss is high; backpropagation adjusts attention weights, embedding tables, and feed-forward layers so similar contexts do better next time.

Everything else (reasoning-like answers, translation, coding help) emerges from getting very good at this one prediction game across diverse text.

3. Where Does the Training Data Come From?

Pretraining data is mostly text scraped from the public internet and curated collections:

  • Websites, encyclopedias, news, forums, blogs
  • Books and academic papers (where licensing allows)
  • Public code repositories
  • Reference material, Q&A threads, documentation

Scale snapshot GPT-3 was trained on roughly 300 billion tokens. Modern frontier models often train on trillions of tokens, with many passes over filtered, deduplicated subsets of the web.

Quantity alone is not enough. Training teams invest heavily in data quality:

  • Filtering removes spam, boilerplate, and low-quality pages
  • Deduplication makes sure the same article copied 10,000 times does not count 10,000 times
  • Safety filtering reduces blatantly harmful or toxic content where possible
  • Mixing balances code, prose, dialogue, and factual text so the model sees varied styles

Bad data teaches bad habits. A model trained mostly on garbled forum spam will produce garbled forum spam, even with billions of parameters.

4. Pretraining: The Expensive, Slow Phase

Pretraining is the first and largest training stage. The model reads huge swaths of text and repeatedly adjusts its billions of parameters to minimize next-token prediction error.

  • Duration: weeks to months on large GPU/TPU clusters
  • Cost: millions to tens of millions of dollars for frontier models
  • Output: a base model, strong at continuing text, but not necessarily helpful or safe to chat with

A base model is like a very well-read autocomplete engine. It has absorbed grammar, facts, coding patterns, and style, but it was never explicitly taught to follow instructions or refuse harmful requests.

5. From Base Model to Assistant: Fine-Tuning

A raw pretrained model continues text. Ask it a question, and it might respond with more questions, random forum posts, or an incomplete sentence, because that is what "predict the next token" looked like on the internet.

Fine-tuning adds a second training phase on curated examples of desired behavior, often prompt/response pairs written by humans or filtered from high-quality conversations:

  • User asks a question → model should give a direct, helpful answer
  • User requests a format → model should follow it (bullets, JSON, short summary)
  • User asks for something unsafe → model should decline politely

Fine-tuning is much cheaper than pretraining because the dataset is tiny by comparison and training runs for far fewer steps. You are not re-reading the whole internet; you are steering an already-capable model toward a product experience.

6. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF, Conceptual)

Even after fine-tuning, two answers can both be "valid" but one is clearer, kinder, or more accurate. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) adds a preference layer:

  1. Show humans several model responses to the same prompt
  2. Ask them to rank responses from best to worst
  3. Train a reward model to predict those human rankings
  4. Nudge the LLM to produce outputs the reward model scores highly

You do not need the RL math details here. The takeaway is simple: RLHF teaches the model what humans prefer, not just what text is most likely on the internet.

Why it matters: Pretraining data includes rude comments, misinformation, and toxic patterns. RLHF helps the deployed assistant be more polite, helpful, and cautious, even when pretraining exposed it to worse examples.

7. Why Scale Matters (Scaling Laws)

Researchers discovered something surprising: for a fixed architecture family, bigger models + more training data + more compute tend to produce smoothly better performance. Plot loss against model size and you see a predictable downward curve, the famous scaling laws.

What the scaling laws actually say

"Scaling laws" refers to two main results. Both describe the model's loss (how wrong its next-token predictions are) rather than benchmark scores, because loss can be measured precisely.

1. Loss falls as a power law (Kaplan et al., OpenAI, 2020). When a model is not held back by the other two factors, its loss depends on the number of parameters \(N\), the number of training tokens \(D\), and the training compute \(C\) in the same simple way:

\[ L(N) \approx \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{0.076} \qquad L(D) \approx \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{0.095} \qquad L(C) \approx \left(\frac{C_c}{C}\right)^{0.050} \]

Here \(N_c\), \(D_c\), and \(C_c\) are constants fitted from experiments. These are power laws: each time you multiply model size by the same factor, loss drops by the same percentage. Because the exponents are small, the gains are steady but expensive. Making a model 10× bigger cuts loss by only about 16%, since \(10^{-0.076} \approx 0.84\). Plotted on log-scale axes, each law is a straight line, and these lines held across more than seven orders of magnitude of model size. That predictability is what lets a lab test small models cheaply and forecast how a large one will perform before spending millions to train it.

2. Grow data as fast as model size (Hoffmann et al., DeepMind, 2022, the "Chinchilla" paper). Training compute is roughly

\[ C \approx 6 \, N \, D \]

floating-point operations, so a fixed budget forces a trade-off between a bigger model and more training data. The Chinchilla study found that for the lowest loss at a given budget, parameters and tokens should grow in equal proportion, at about 20 training tokens per parameter. Many earlier models were far too large for the amount of data they saw. DeepMind showed this by training Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4 trillion tokens) with the same compute as their earlier Gopher (280B parameters, 300 billion tokens). Chinchilla was 4× smaller and outperformed Gopher on nearly every benchmark.

Worked example: GPT-3. GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters and was trained on 300 billion tokens. Its training compute was about \(6 \times 175\text{B} \times 300\text{B} \approx 3 \times 10^{23}\) operations. That is under 2 tokens per parameter. By the Chinchilla rule, a model that size calls for about \(20 \times 175\text{B} = 3.5\) trillion tokens, more than 10× what GPT-3 actually saw.

How labs use these laws today. The Chinchilla rule minimizes the cost of training. But a popular model then answers millions of requests, and a smaller model is cheaper and faster for every one of them (the next two lessons cover this cost). So many recent models are deliberately trained well past 20 tokens per parameter. Meta's Llama 3 8B, for example, was trained on about 15 trillion tokens, close to 1,900 tokens per parameter. Training costs more up front, but the result is a small model that performs like a much larger one.

Check your understanding: Chinchilla

Question: You have the budget to train a 10-billion-parameter model. Using the Chinchilla rule, about how many training tokens should you use, and roughly how much compute will training take?

Answer: About \(20 \times 10\text{B} = 200\) billion tokens, and \(C \approx 6 \times 10\text{B} \times 200\text{B} = 1.2 \times 10^{22}\) operations.

Nobody designed the model to "learn reasoning" as a separate module. Yet as scale increased, systems started passing exams, writing code, and translating languages, skills that were not explicitly labeled in the training data. That emergent behavior is a major reason labs keep investing in scale, even though it is expensive.

Scale is not infinite magic. Data runs out, costs explode, and diminishing returns appear. But the scaling-law mindset explains why GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and Llama 3 announcements all emphasize parameters, training tokens, and compute budget.

8. Try It: Spot the Difference

Same prompt, two responses. One comes from a base / completion-style model; the other from an instruction-tuned assistant. Read both, guess which is which, then check your answer.

Prompt:

Explain photosynthesis in two sentences for a 10-year-old.

Response A

Explain photosynthesis in two sentences for a 10-year-old. Explain photosynthesis simply. What is photosynthesis? Photosynthesis is a process used by plants. Here are some related questions you might ask…

Response B

Plants eat sunlight! They use it to turn water and air into sugar food and give us fresh oxygen to breathe. Without photosynthesis, Earth would not have the green plants or the oxygen we need.

Which response is from the instruction-tuned assistant?

Correct. Response B follows the instruction (two simple sentences, kid-friendly tone). Response A keeps continuing the prompt like a autocomplete engine, repeating the question and listing meta-commentary instead of answering directly. That pattern is typical of base models before instruction fine-tuning.
Not quite. Look for which response actually answers the question in two sentences. Base models often repeat or extend the prompt; instruction-tuned models try to fulfill the request.
