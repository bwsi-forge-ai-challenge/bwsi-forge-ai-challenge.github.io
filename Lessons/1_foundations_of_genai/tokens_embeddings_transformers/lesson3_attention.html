Attention: The Key Innovation

Attention lets a model connect each word to the other words in a sentence that help explain it. This lesson builds it up from the intuition to the math of queries, keys and values.

Think about reading a story. Early on, you're introduced to a character named Alice. Dozens of pages later, you read the sentence, "She opened the door." Without thinking about it, you know that "she" refers to Alice. Even though many pages separate the two, your brain quickly connects them.

Language is full of these long-distance connections. We naturally figure out which words relate to one another and use that context to understand what's being said.

In this lesson, we'll see how computers can learn to do something similar. The idea that makes this possible is called attention, and it's one of the biggest breakthroughs in modern AI. Attention is the key idea behind Transformers, the neural network design that powers models like ChatGPT and many of today's most advanced AI systems. The next lesson defines transformers fully and shows how attention fits inside one.

1. Let's Start With a Problem

Take a look at this sentence:

"The bank executive jumped over the bank wall."

The word bank appears twice, but it doesn't mean the same thing both times.

  • The first bank refers to a financial institution. The nearby word executive gives you that clue.
  • The second bank refers to the edge of a river. This time, words like wall suggest a riverbank instead.

You probably figured that out immediately. But notice what your brain actually did. You didn't interpret the word bank by itself. You looked at the surrounding words to figure out which meaning made sense.

The challenge: The meaning of a word depends on its context. If an AI only looks at one word at a time, or only pays attention to a few nearby words, it can easily misunderstand the sentence. We need a way for every word to find and use the other words that matter, even if they're far apart.

2. Why Couldn't The RNN Model Handle This?

Previously, you learned about Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). An RNN reads a sentence one word at a time, moving from left to right while carrying a hidden "memory" of everything it has seen so far.

That sounds reasonable, but it creates a problem. When the model reaches the first bank, it has only read:

The → bank

At that moment, it hasn't seen the word executive yet. Since that important clue comes later in the sentence, the model has to guess what "bank" means without all the information.

There's another challenge. Imagine the sentence were much longer. By the time an RNN reaches the end, it has to remember information from dozens, or even hundreds, of words earlier. As that information is passed from one step to the next, some of it can gradually fade or become less useful.

This is closely related to a training issue called the vanishing gradient problem, which you learned about in a previous lesson. In practice, it makes learning long-range relationships much more difficult.

Attention takes a completely different approach. Instead of relying on a memory that gets passed from one word to the next, every word can directly look at the other words in the sentence whenever it needs more context. It doesn't matter whether those words are right next to each other or many words apart.

3. The Big Idea: Every Word Asks a Question

Before we introduce the math behind attention, let's build some intuition.

When the model processes each word, imagine that it is asking:

"Which other words in this sentence will help me understand what I mean?"

The model looks at every word in the sentence, decides which ones are most helpful, and combines information from those words to better understand the one it's currently processing.

Let's go back to our example:

"The bank executive jumped."

Suppose the model is trying to understand the word bank. Rather than treating it as an isolated word, it compares bank with every other word in the sentence.

  1. It asks, "Which words will help me figure out what 'bank' means here?"
  2. It looks at every word: The, bank, executive, and jumped.
  3. It realizes that executive is a very useful clue because executives usually work at financial institutions.
  4. It gives very little attention to The, since articles don't add much meaning, and some attention to jumped, which describes an action but doesn't help identify the type of bank.
  5. Finally, it combines information from all of these words (giving more weight to the important ones) to build a better representation of bank.

Notice that the word itself hasn't changed. It's still the word bank. What has changed is the model's understanding of that word.

In this sentence, bank now carries the meaning of a financial institution, not the edge of a river. If we placed the same word in a different sentence, attention would focus on different surrounding words and arrive at a different meaning.

That's the power of attention: instead of assigning every word a single fixed meaning, it allows the model to understand words in context.

4. Turning the Idea Into Math (Gently)

Now that you have the intuition, let's see how a computer actually performs attention. The good news is that the process is much simpler than it might sound. We'll build it one step at a time.

Step 1: Every word gets three different vectors

Recall that prior to being passed to the network, every word is first converted into an embedding, which is a vector that captures its meaning. Attention starts by taking that embedding and creating three new vectors. Each one has a different purpose.

Vector Purpose Intuition
Query (Q) What information am I looking for? Like typing a search into Google. The word asks, "Who can help me understand my meaning?"
Key (K) What kind of information do I provide? Like a webpage title that helps others decide whether it's relevant.
Value (V) What information should I contribute? Like the actual contents of the webpage after you click on it.

Every word in the sentence creates its own Query, Key, and Value vectors. The model learns how to produce these vectors during training.

For a sequence of word embeddings collected in a matrix X, the three vector types are computed with learned weight matrices (\(W_Q, W_K, W_V\)):

\[Q = XW_Q, K = XW_K, V = XW_V\]

Each row of Q, K, and V belongs to one token in the sentence.

Step 2: Compare the Query with every Key

Next, the model measures how well each word matches every other word.

Suppose we're trying to understand the word bank. Its Query vector is compared with the Key vector of every word in the sentence, including its own. Each comparison produces a score that tells the model how useful that word might be.

Mathematically, this comparison is a dot product between two vectors. For token i looking at token j:

\[s_{ij} = \mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j\]

A larger score \(s_{ij}\) means token \(j\) looks more useful for understanding token \(i\).

Step 3: Turn the scores into attention weights

After comparing every pair of words, the model has a list of scores. Some are large, some are small, and some may even be negative.

To make these scores easier to use, they're passed through a function called softmax. Softmax converts them into values between 0 and 1 that add up to 1, so they can be interpreted as how much attention each word should receive.

This is the same softmax function defined in the MLP prerequisite. In a classifier, softmax converts raw scores into class probabilities. Here, it converts relevance scores into attention weights that sum to 1 for each row. First divide each score by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) (the size of each Key vector). Call the result a scaled score \(s'_{ij}\):

\[s'_{ij} = \frac{s_{ij}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\] \[\alpha_{ij} = \frac{e^{s'_{ij}}}{Z_i}\] \[Z_i = \sum_{j} e^{s'_{ij}}\]

Each weight \(\alpha_{ij}\) is between 0 and 1, and for a fixed row i the weights add up to 1. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the dot products from growing too large (this scaling appears in the original Transformer paper).

Step 4: Combine information from the important words

Finally, the model uses those attention weights to combine the Value vectors. Words with higher attention weights contribute more to the final representation, while less relevant words contribute very little.

The result is a new representation of the word that includes information from the rest of the sentence. Instead of relying only on its original embedding, the word now carries the context it needs to be understood correctly.

\[\mathbf{z}_i = \sum_{j} \alpha_{ij} \mathbf{v}_j\]

Each output vector \(z_i\) is a weighted blend of all Value vectors, with weights set by attention.

All four steps are often written as one formula: \[ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left( \frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) V \]

1. Create Query, Key, and Value vectors
2. Compare each Query with every Key (dot product)
3. Apply softmax to get attention weights
4. Compute a weighted combination of the Value vectors

Those four steps are the entire attention mechanism. In the next section, we'll work through a concrete numerical example so you can see exactly how each step is calculated.

5. Walking Through a Real Example

Let's see what attention looks like in practice using the sentence:

"The bank executive jumped."

We'll focus on the word bank. Remember, the goal is to figure out which other words are most helpful for understanding what bank means in this sentence.

Step 1: Compute relevance scores

The model compares the Query for bank with the Key for every word in the sentence. This produces a relevance score for each word.

Word Relevance Score Why?
The 0.3 Not very informative. It's a common article that doesn't tell us much about the meaning of bank.
bank (itself) 1.0 The word itself is naturally important, so it receives a high score.
executive 0.8 A strong clue that we're talking about a financial institution rather than a riverbank.
jumped 0.2 This describes an action, but it doesn't say much about what kind of bank we're referring to.

These aren't probabilities yet; they're simply similarity scores. Larger scores mean the model thinks that word is more useful for understanding bank.

Step 2: Convert the scores into attention weights

Next, the scores are passed through softmax, which converts them into attention weights that add up to 1.

weights ≈ [0.15, 0.40, 0.35, 0.10]

Now we can interpret these numbers more easily. About 40% of the final representation comes from bank itself, about 35% comes from executive, and the remaining words contribute much smaller amounts.

Step 3: Combine the Value vectors

Finally, the model uses those weights to combine the Value vectors for every word.

Output = 0.15 × Value(The)
        + 0.40 × Value(bank)
        + 0.35 × Value(executive)
        + 0.10 × Value(jumped)

The result is a brand-new vector representing the word bank. This vector doesn't just capture the dictionary meaning of the word; it captures what bank means in this particular sentence.

Because executive received a large attention weight, the new representation is pulled toward the meaning of a financial institution. If the sentence had been "The fisherman sat on the bank," attention would have focused on different words, producing a different representation for exactly the same word.

This is what makes attention so powerful: the meaning of a word can change depending on its context, and the model automatically learns which surrounding words matter most.

Summary: What Attention Is Trying to Do

Attention gives every word a way to look at the rest of the sentence and ask: Which other words should shape my meaning right now? It turns that question into numbers so the model can represent the same word differently in different sentences.

Why It Matters

  • Context: Ambiguous words like bank can mean different things depending on neighbors like executive or river.
  • Long-range links: Pronouns like She can connect to Alice even when many words sit between them.
  • Parallel processing: Unlike RNNs, all words can be updated together, which is faster to train on modern hardware.
  • Foundation of Transformers: This mechanism, plus the two additions in the next sections, is the core idea behind models like ChatGPT.

You do not need to memorize every symbol. The key takeaway: attention = weighted lookup over the sentence, learned from data.

6. Why Does Attention Work So Well?

Attention solved several problems that had limited earlier language models. Here are some of the biggest advantages.

  • It can process an entire sentence at once. Unlike an RNN, which reads one word after another, attention allows every word in the sentence to be processed simultaneously. This makes training much faster and allows modern AI models to take advantage of powerful GPUs.
  • Long-distance relationships are easy to capture. A word can directly attend to another word anywhere in the sentence, whether it's one word away or one hundred. The model doesn't have to pass information step by step through a long chain of words.
  • The model learns what matters on its own. Nobody manually tells the model that words like executive or money should be associated with a financial bank. By training on enormous amounts of text, it gradually discovers these relationships for itself.
  • Words can have different meanings in different situations. The word bank doesn't always produce the same representation. In one sentence it may represent a financial institution, while in another it represents the side of a river. Attention uses the surrounding context to determine which meaning fits best.

7. Looking at a Sentence from More Than One Perspective

Up to this point, our attention mechanism has relied on a single set of Query, Key, and Value weights (\(W_Q, W_K, W_V\)). In machine learning terms, that setup is called a single attention head.

Real-world transformer architectures don't rely on just one; they run several heads simultaneously side by side. This setup is known as multi-head attention. To understand why researchers introduced this layer of complexity, look at where a single head runs into a wall.

The Limitation of a Single Head

Consider this classic example:

"The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired."

To make sense of the word "it," a language model needs to resolve several questions at once:

  • Coreference: What does "it" refer to? (animal, not street)
  • Predicate/State: What condition is being described? (was too tired)
  • Grammatical Role: How does it function syntactically? (subject of the verb was)

With only one attention head, the model gets a single distribution of attention weights for "it," and those weights must sum up to \(1.0\). If the head allocates \(0.70\) of its attention budget to animal, only \(0.30\) remains for every other token in the sequence.

Because the resulting context vector is just a weighted sum of Value vectors, these distinct linguistic roles get smeared together into an undifferentiated average. Even worse, with only one \(W_Q\) and one \(W_K\), the model can only define "relevance" along a single dimension at any given moment.

The Solution: Multiple Parallel Heads

Multi-head attention solves this bottleneck by giving each head its own unique weight matrices: Head 1 uses \((W_Q^{(1)}, W_K^{(1)}, W_V^{(1)})\), Head 2 uses \((W_Q^{(2)}, W_K^{(2)}, W_V^{(2)})\), and so on.

Each head calculates its own Queries, Keys, and Values, scores token pairs independently, applies softmax, and creates its own contextualized representation. Because their weights differ, each head develops its own definition of relevance and tracks different patterns across the sentence.

Here is an intuitive snapshot of how two heads might break down the token "it":

Attention HeadPrimary Attention TargetSyntactic / Semantic Role Captured
Head 1animal (\(0.80\))Coreference: Identifies the noun being referenced.
Head 2tired (\(0.55\)), was (\(0.30\))State: Captures the condition being described.

Neither head has to compromise. One head focuses on tracking who the subject is, while another tracks what that subject is doing or feeling.

Bringing the Heads Back Together

Once each head computes its own output vector for a word, the model needs to collapse those parallel perspectives back into a single vector before moving downstream.

The mechanism is straightforward:

  1. Concatenate: Line up the output vectors from every head side by side into one long vector.
  2. Project: Multiply that concatenated vector by an additional learned weight matrix, \(W_O\).
\[\text{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h)\,W_O\]

The projection matrix \(W_O\) acts like an editor, blending the independent findings from each head into a unified, rich word representation.

More Heads Without the Computational Penalty

It might seem like running 8 or 12 attention heads would multiply the computational workload by 8 or 12. In practice, it doesn't.

Instead of computing giant attention maps across the full embedding size, transformers divide the embedding dimensions evenly across the heads:

\[\text{Head Dimension } (d_k) = \frac{d_{\text{model}}}{\text{Number of Heads } (h)}\]

Take GPT-2 Small as a concrete example:

  • Full model embedding dimension (\(d_{\text{model}}\)): \(768\)
  • Total heads (\(h\)): \(12\)
  • Dimension per head (\(d_k\)): \(768 \div 12 = 64\)

Each head calculates its Query, Key, and Value vectors in a compact \(64\)-dimensional space. Concatenating the outputs of all 12 heads yields \(12 \times 64 = 768\) numbers, matching the original model dimension. The model isn't doing 12 times the work; it is dividing a single high-dimensional space into 12 smaller, specialized subspaces.

What Do the Heads Actually Specialize In?

Engineers don't handcraft duties for individual heads. At initialization, their weights are set randomly. As the model trains on massive text corpora through gradient descent, the heads naturally diverge to capture distinct linguistic features that minimize next-token prediction loss.

When researchers inspect trained architectures (such as BERT), they frequently observe recurring specializations:

  • Positional Heads: Heads that consistently attend to the immediate previous or next token (Clark et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019).
  • Coreference Heads: Heads that link pronouns (she, they, it) back to their antecedents across clauses (Clark et al., 2019).
  • Syntactic Heads: Heads that map direct objects to transitive verbs, or verbs to their subjects, regardless of how many intervening words separate them (Clark et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019).

Interestingly, ablation studies show that not every head carries equal weight. Some heads perform subtle statistical work that resists straightforward human interpretation, and many can be pruned away post-training with minimal drop in performance (Voita et al., 2019). The specialization isn't programmed in advance; it emerges out of optimization.

Quick Concept Check

Q1: Suppose a model uses an embedding size of \(512\) with \(8\) attention heads. What is the dimension of each individual head's Query vector?

A: \(512 \div 8 = 64\) dimensions.

Q2: Why not just keep one attention head and make its Query and Key vectors much larger?

A: Expanding vector dimensions within a single head doesn't change the underlying math: a single head produces only one set of attention weights per token, and those weights must sum to \(1.0\). Larger vectors let a model evaluate more complex features along one plane, but they cannot allocate full attention to two separate words for two independent syntactic reasons simultaneously. Multiple heads provide separate attention budgets.

8. The Missing Piece: How Transformers Understand Word Order

Attention does an exceptional job calculating which tokens relate to one another. But on its own, standard self-attention has a glaring blind spot: it is completely permutation-invariant. It has no built-in awareness of sequence order.

Why Pure Attention Ignores Sequence Order

Recall how an attention score is calculated between two tokens:

\[\text{Score}(q_i, k_j) = q_i \cdot k_j^T\]

The Query and Key vectors depend strictly on their respective token embeddings. The embedding for "dog" is looked up from the exact same row in the vocabulary matrix whether it sits at the beginning, middle, or end of a sequence.

Consider how self-attention processes these two distinct sentences:

  1. "The dog bit the cat."
  2. "The cat bit the dog."

Look at the verb "bit." In both sentences, the Query vector for bit is compared against the Key vectors for the identical set of vocabulary items: the, dog, the, and cat.

Because dot products depend purely on vector values and not sequence position, the resulting set of attention weights will be identical, merely reshuffled in order. When the Value vectors are weighted and summed, the final context vector for bit turns out identical in both sentences.

Without an intervention, the model cannot tell who bit whom.

Traditional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) didn't face this issue because they ingested tokens sequentially, updating a hidden state one step at a time (\(t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n\)). Transformers achieve massive parallelism by processing all tokens at once, but that means word order must be injected explicitly.

The Solution: Positional Encodings

To give the model a sense of order, we assign a distinct coordinate vector, called a positional encoding (\(P_i\)), to every position index in the sequence.

Before the input enters the first self-attention layer, the token's semantic embedding (\(E_i\)) is summed element-wise with its positional vector (\(P_i\)):

\[\text{Input Vector}_i = E_i + P_i\] 
   Word Embedding (E_i):   [ 0.35, -0.12,  0.88, ... ]  (What the token means)
+  Position Vector (P_i):  [ 0.02,  0.94, -0.41, ... ]  (Where the token is)
------------------------------------------------------
=  Final Input:            [ 0.37,  0.82,  0.47, ... ]  (Context-ready token)

Because the position vector is added rather than concatenated, the input dimension stays unchanged, preserving the model's footprint. During training, the transformer learns to decouple semantic features from spatial coordinates directly from this combined vector.

Now, "dog" at index 2 and "dog" at index 5 enter the attention layer as distinct numerical vectors, generating distinct Queries, Keys, and Values. The attention scores naturally reflect both what a word means and where it sits.

How Positional Vectors Are Generated

Modern architectures typically rely on one of two methods to construct positional encodings:

MethodHow It WorksKey CharacteristicsUsed By
Learned EmbeddingsUses a dedicated lookup matrix where each position index (\(0, 1, \dots, N\)) has its own trainable vector.Simple to optimize; bounded by a fixed maximum context length (\(N\)).GPT-2, BERT
Sinusoidal FunctionsGenerates static position vectors using continuous sine and cosine functions across varying wavelengths.Deterministic (no parameters to train); mathematically extrapolates to unseen lengths.Original Transformer (Attention Is All You Need)

The Intuition Behind Sinusoidal Encodings

The sinusoidal formulation from Vaswani et al. (2017) defines the values of each dimension across positions using paired sine and cosine functions:

\[PE_{(pos,\, 2i)} = \sin\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right)\] \[PE_{(pos,\, 2i+1)} = \cos\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right)\]

Where \(pos\) represents the position index in the sequence, and \(i\) represents the dimension index within the vector.

The underlying intuition is analogous to an analog clock face:

  • The second hand rotates rapidly, completing a cycle every 60 seconds. It provides high precision for telling adjacent seconds apart.
  • The minute hand moves moderately, completing a cycle once an hour.
  • The hour hand moves slowly, completing a cycle across 12 hours, distinguishing broader blocks of time.

Sinusoidal positional encodings operate on the same principle. Lower vector dimensions oscillate at high frequencies (short wavelengths), varying sharply between neighboring words. Higher dimensions oscillate at much lower frequencies (long wavelengths), changing gradually across long stretches of context.

Read together across all dimensions, every position index receives a unique numerical fingerprint. Furthermore, because trigonometric functions follow linear transformation identities (\(\cos(\alpha + \beta)\), \(\sin(\alpha + \beta)\)), this setup makes it straightforward for self-attention to learn relative distances between tokens regardless of their absolute positions in the text.
