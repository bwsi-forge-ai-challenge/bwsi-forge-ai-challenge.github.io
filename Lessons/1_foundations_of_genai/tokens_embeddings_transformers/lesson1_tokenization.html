Tokenization: How Computers Learn to Read

When you open ChatGPT or any large language model, it feels like having a conversation with an articulate human. You type a sentence, and it replies with a complete, nuanced thought.

Behind that polished interface, however, sits a giant mathematical equation.

Computers do not understand humor, poetry, or grammar. At the hardware level, neural networks are essentially gigantic calculators running billions of matrix multiplications on floating-point numbers. They have no concept of what the letter "c" or the word "cat" looks like. They only understand math.

So, how do we bridge the gap between human language and raw arithmetic? That translation step is called tokenization.

1. The Core Problem: Turning Text Into Numbers

Before an AI model can generate or predict anything, it needs an entry door that converts text strings into numbers.

The Naive Attempt: One Number Per Letter

Suppose we try the simplest approach: assign each letter a number.

a = 1, b = 2, c = 3 ... z = 26

Using this system, the word "cat" becomes [3, 1, 20].

While this works mathematically, it creates two major headaches for machine learning:

  • Letters carry almost zero standalone meaning. The number 3 represents "c", but "c" tells the model nothing about whether we are talking about a cat, a car, or a cloud.
  • Sequences get enormous. A standard 500-word essay easily stretches into 2,500 individual character steps. Because modern neural networks compare every position in a sequence against every other position, super-long sequences explode computational costs and memory requirements.

The Opposite Extreme: One Number Per Word

What if we assign a unique ID to every complete word instead?

"apple" = 101
"cat" = 102
"run" = 103

This keeps sequences compact. "The cat sat" is just three numbers. But real human language breaks this quickly:

  • The Dictionary Becomes Infinite: English alone contains hundreds of thousands of words. Add plurals ("cats"), verb tenses ("running", "ran"), compound words, and slang, and your vocabulary dictionary spirals into millions of entries. Models cannot efficiently manage a dictionary of that size.
  • The "Out-of-Vocabulary" (OOV) Trap: What happens when someone types a typo ("univeristy"), a brand name, or a new piece of slang? If the word is not already in the model's fixed dictionary, the model has to throw its hands up and replace it with <UNK> (Unknown), wiping out all intended meaning.

We need a middle ground: chunks large enough to carry rich meaning, yet flexible enough to spell out any word without crashing.

2. Meet the Token: A Reusable Chunk of Meaning

A token is simply a chunk of text that a model reads as a single unit. It can be an entire common word, a partial word piece, a punctuation mark, or even a single character.

Consider the word "unhappily":

  • A whole-word system sees: ["unhappily"] (1 token, but requires memorizing every variation of happy).
  • A character system sees: ["u", "n", "h", "a", "p", "p", "i", "l", "y"] (9 tokens, long and tedious).
  • A modern subword system sees: ["un", "happ", "ily"] (3 tokens).

Notice how intuitive that subword split is. The piece "un" appears in thousands of words ("undo", "unreal", "unfair") to signal negation. The piece "happ" links directly to "happy" and "happiness". The suffix "ily" signals an adverb ("easily", "greedily").

By breaking words into reusable building blocks, the AI captures grammatical patterns and meaning without needing an endless dictionary.

3. How Subword Tokenization Works (Byte-Pair Encoding)

Modern language models rely heavily on an algorithm called Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) to discover these building blocks automatically from raw text.

Instead of linguists manually deciding which prefixes and suffixes to save, BPE learns by finding statistical patterns across massive collections of text:

  1. Start small: Treat every individual character and byte as the initial vocabulary.
  2. Count pairs: Scan the entire dataset and count how often any two neighboring tokens appear side-by-side.
  3. Merge the winner: Take the single most common pair and glue them together into a brand-new token. For example, if "t" and "h" appear together millions of times, merge them into "th".
  4. Repeat: Continue finding and merging the most frequent neighboring pairs over thousands of cycles until the vocabulary reaches a predetermined target cap (typically between 32,000 and 100,000 unique tokens).

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • High-frequency words like "the", "and", or "banana" appear so often during training that the algorithm merges them into complete, single tokens.
  • Uncommon or complex words merge partially into intuitive roots and affixes ("un" + "happ" + "ily").
  • Brand-new or fabricated words like "unxyz" never crash the system. The model simply uses the familiar prefix "un" alongside the smaller characters "x", "y", and "z".

4. The Two-Step Pipeline: Text to IDs

Tokenization operates as a strict two-stage process:

  1. Text to String Chunks: The tokenizer slices raw text into specific token strings.
  2. String Chunks to Integer IDs: Every token in the model's vocabulary has a fixed, unique index in a lookup dictionary. The tokenizer swaps each text piece for its corresponding integer ID.

For example, the phrase "The cat sat on the mat" passes through the pipeline like this:

StepRepresentation
Raw Text"The cat sat on the mat"
Tokens["The", " cat", " sat", " on", " the", " mat"]
Token IDs[50, 3277, 2521, 319, 50, 10152]

(Notice the leading spaces attached to some tokens. To a tokenizer, spaces are characters just like letters, so " cat" with a space and "cat" without a space receive different IDs.)

This mapping is entirely deterministic. For any specific tokenizer model, the exact same string will always map to the exact same sequence of numbers.

5. Why Tokenization Matters in Practice

Tokenization is not just an obscure engineering detail under the hood. It sets the baseline rules for how models behave, what they cost, and what they struggle with:

API Pricing

Providers like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google charge based on the number of tokens processed and generated. Token count is literally the meter on the pump. In English, a general rule of thumb is that 1,000 tokens equal roughly 750 words.

The "Language Tax"

Because tokenizers are trained primarily on internet data, which is heavily English-dominant, common English words usually count as single tokens. Less-represented languages (or languages that do not use the Latin alphabet, like Japanese, Arabic, or Hindi) often break down into multiple tokens per word or even multiple tokens per character.

Saying the exact same sentence can take 2 to 3 times more tokens in Japanese than in English. This means non-English speakers often pay more for the same underlying information.

6. Tokenization Quirks and AI Blindspots

Because tokenizers look purely for repeated character sequences rather than true conceptual understanding, they introduce strange behaviors that catch people off guard:

  • Why AIs Struggle with Math: When you type "1234567890", the tokenizer might chop it into arbitrary fragments like ["123", "4567", "890"]. The model never sees a clean sequence of individual columns (ones, tens, hundreds). It sees three awkward numerical chunks, making basic arithmetic tricky.
  • Why AIs Struggle to Count Letters: If you ask a model "How many 'r's are in 'strawberry'?", it frequently answers wrong. To the model, "strawberry" is simply two tokens: ["straw", "berry"]. It never inspects the raw letters unless explicitly prompted to spell the word out character-by-character.
  • The ALL CAPS Penalty: Words written in ALL CAPS appear less frequently in typical books and training articles. As a result, the tokenizer often fails to keep them whole. Typing "hello" is 1 token, but typing "HELLO" can shatter into ["H", "EL", "LO"], forcing the model to do extra work.
  • Formatting and Curly Quotes: A straight quote typed on your keyboard (') and a curly quote generated by a word processor (’) look different to a tokenizer. Pasting styled text from an app like Google Docs or Word can trigger unexpected token splits that alter how the model processes your prompt.
