Tokenization: Breaking Text Into Pieces

The what_is_genai unit explained what generative models do. This unit opens the hood: text must become numbers before a neural network can process it. Tokenization is that first step — and its design choices ripple through training cost, context limits, multilingual behavior, and even what the model finds "easy" to learn.

1. The Problem: Models Need Numbers

Neural networks are math machines. They multiply matrices of floating-point numbers. You cannot feed the letter a directly into a matrix multiply.

First step: convert text → numbers.

Naive idea: one number per letter

Map a=1, b=2, …, z=26. The word "cat" becomes [3, 1, 20].

Problem: letters carry little meaning alone. The sequence [3, 1, 20] also appears inside unrelated strings if you ignore word boundaries. Sequences get long, and the model must learn spelling from scratch every time.

2. Tokens: Chunks of Meaning

A token is a chunk of text — not necessarily a whole word. Each token gets a unique integer ID. Think of tokenizing a book as converting it into a list of word-piece IDs the model's vocabulary understands.

Why chunks, not letters? Words and subwords carry more semantic signal per position. The model sees fewer steps per sentence and can reuse frequent pieces across many words.

3. Tokenization Schemes

How we choose to split this text drastically changes what an AI can learn, how fast it processes information, and how large its "vocabulary dictionary" needs to be.

Word Tokenization

The most intuitive approach is to split text by looking for spaces (whitespace) and punctuation marks. If you read a sentence, you naturally see words as the basic units of meaning.

The Downside: Word tokenization runs into major issues with real-world language:

  • Ambiguity: Is a contraction like don't a single token, or should it be split into three (don, ', t)? What about hyphenated words like state-of-the-art?
  • The Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) Problem: Language constantly evolves. If a student types a brand-new slang word, a brand-new product name, or a fictional name like "Xylora", the model won't find it in its preset dictionary. It forces the system to replace the entire word with a generic <UNK> (Unknown) token, erasing all specific details and meaning.

Character Tokenization

Instead of words, we can break everything down to the absolute absolute basics: individual letters, numbers, and punctuation marks. Every single character becomes its own token.

The Downside: While this completely solves the "unknown word" problem (because any new word is just made of familiar letters), it introduces a massive computational burden:

  • Sequence Explosion: The sentence "The cat sat." is only 3 word tokens, but it expands to 12 character tokens (including spaces and punctuation). For an AI model, processing massive walls of text character-by-character requires a huge amount of memory.
  • Diluted Meaning: Individual characters carry no inherent meaning. The letter 'c' tells you nothing by itself. The AI has to work incredibly hard across many processing steps just to learn that combining the character sequence c-a-t represents a small, furry animal.

Subword Tokenization (Modern Default)

Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) use a smart middle-ground approach. Subword tokenization ensures that frequently used words stay entirely whole, while rare or complex words are systematically broken down into smaller, reusable fragments (like prefixes, suffixes, or roots).

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) Mechanics:

  1. Start by treating every individual character as a baseline token.
  2. Scan the entire text corpus and count how often pairs of tokens appear side-by-side.
  3. Find the single most common adjacent pair and merge them together into a brand-new, single token.
  4. Repeat this loop thousands of times until your total vocabulary reach its target cap size (typically around 32,000 to 50,000 unique tokens).

The Result: Common words like "happy" stay as a single token. A rare variant like "unhappily" automatically splits into reusable components: ["un", "happy", "ly"]. If it encounters a completely fabricated word like "unxyz", it won't crash or throw an unknown token error; it simply handles it cleanly as ["un", "xyz"].

4. GPT-Style Tokenization (Real-World Example)

GPT-family models use BPE on byte sequences (cl100k_base for GPT-4 class models). Vocabulary size ≈ 50,000 tokens.

Example: "Hello, how are you?" might become IDs like [15339, 11, 534, 366, 345, 30] — exact splits depend on the trained merge table.

Key property: tokenization is deterministic. The same string always yields the same token sequence for a fixed tokenizer version.

5. Why Tokenization Matters

  • Downstream effects: embedding lookup, attention length, and memory all depend on token count, not word count.
  • Longer sequences → slower training and inference, higher API cost.
  • Language differences: English averages ~1.3 tokens per word; Japanese may use ~1.3 tokens per character — same meaning can cost 2–3× more tokens.
  • Split entities: if "New" and "York" are separate tokens, the model must learn they belong together (it can, but it's harder).
  • Billing: OpenAI and other APIs charge per token — tokenization is literally the meter on the pump.

6. Edge Cases and Quirks

Because subword tokenizers rely strictly on mathematical patterns found in their training text rather than understanding human rules, they produce strange, non-intuitive quirks. These "glitches" directly affect how an AI counts words, handles formatting, or understands code.

  • Spaces are Regular Characters: A common mistake is thinking tokenizers only look at letters. In reality, spaces are treated just like any other character. In many modern tokenizers (like the one used by GPT models), spaces are represented by a special meta-character (like Ġ or _) and typically attach themselves to the beginning of the following word.
    Example: "hello world" splits into ["hello", " world"]. The space belongs to " world", meaning an AI considers "world" and " world" as two completely separate tokens in its dictionary.

  • The Math Blindspot (Number Splitting): Tokenizers are notoriously bad at math because of how they slice numbers. Short, common numbers like "123" or "2026" are often bundled into a single whole token. However, larger or rare numbers get broken into unpredictable fragments.
    Example: The number "1234567890" might be chopped up into random pieces like ["123", "4567", "890"]. Because the AI doesn't see the digits sequentially, it struggles with basic arithmetic on larger numbers.

  • Emoji Math & Mishaps: An emoji might look like a single cute symbol on your screen, but behind the scenes, it is made up of complex bytes of data. Depending on how old or advanced the tokenizer is, an emoji might be preserved as a single token, or completely shredded into raw data fragments.
    Example: A basic smile might be one token, but a complex family emoji with specific skin tone modifiers might explode into 4 or 5 separate tokens, causing the AI to pay "extra" attention to a single character.

  • The Typographic Trap (Straight vs. Curly Quotes): To an AI, a straight apostrophe typed on a standard keyboard (') is completely unrelated to a stylized, curly apostrophe automatically generated by word processors (’).
    Example: The word "don't" might map cleanly to 2 tokens, while "don’t" with a curly quote might shatter into ["don", "â", "Ģ", "trademark symbol", "t"]. Copy-pasting text from a Google Doc can accidentally change how an AI interprets a sentence.

  • ALL CAPS Penalty: LLM training text is mostly standard, grammatically correct prose. Because words written in ALL CAPS are relatively rare in books and articles, the tokenizer hasn't learned to keep them whole.
    Example: The lowercase word "hello" is a single, common token. If an angry user types "HELLO", the tokenizer might split it into ["H", "EL", "LO"]. Typing in all caps forces the AI to process more tokens, leaving less room in its memory for actual reasoning.

8. Activities

  1. Tokenize your name. How many tokens? Does it split into subwords? Why?
  2. Try another language. Is the token count longer than English? Why?
  3. Find an odd split. Paste a URL, hashtag, or rare word — why did the tokenizer break it that way?
  4. Challenge: Find a short string that uses the most tokens per character.
  5. Back-of-envelope: A 77,000-word book × ~1.3 tokens/word ≈ 100,000 tokens (Harry Potter book 1 scale). At $0.01/1k tokens, one full read-through generation costs ~$1 in tokens alone.
