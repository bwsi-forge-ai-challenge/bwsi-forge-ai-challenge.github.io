Running Experiments You Can Trust

One impressive output proves very little about a generative model. These systems are random and occasionally brilliant, so you can tell almost any story by cherry-picking. This lesson covers fair baselines, ablations, honest error bars, human evaluation, and learning from failures.

1. Fair Comparisons

A metric alone means nothing: “our captions got a CLIPScore of 31” only matters next to another system on the exact same inputs. That is your baseline, and for a fine-tuned model the one that counts is the base model with a good prompt (Fine-Tuning for a Style). Change one variable at a time, and lock everything else:

  • Use the same held-out inputs, chosen before you look at any outputs.
  • Keep generation settings identical (temperature, guidance scale, step count).
  • Fix random seeds so both systems start from the same noise.

One seed can be lucky or unlucky, so run several seeds over a batch of inputs and report the average and the spread.

Predict before you run. Write down what you expect and why, for example “LoRA rank 4 will sound closer to the base model than rank 32, because it has less capacity.” Afterward, hindsight makes any result look explainable; a wrong prediction is the most useful outcome, because it shows your mental model was off. Keep a dated log of predictions and results.

2. Ablations: Which Part Mattered?

An ablation removes or reverts one component at a time and measures what changes. In a multi-step pipeline, a leave-one-out analysis swaps one fine-tuned step back to its base model, leaving the rest intact, and shows how the end-to-end score shifts. For a pipeline that captions a photo (A), rewrites the caption into an image prompt (B) and generates a picture (C), the CLIP similarity between the final image and the original photo is:

The rewriter barely matters (+0.02), and a shift that small could be noise. Also remember that stages interact, so swapping two at once need not equal the sum of swapping each.

3. How Sure Are You? Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

Say 8 generated images score 0.71, 0.64, 0.80, 0.58, 0.75, 0.69, 0.62, 0.77 against their originals. The mean is 0.695, but a different batch of 8 could easily give 0.66 or 0.73. A 95% confidence interval puts a range around that uncertainty. The bootstrap computes one without heavy math, by treating your sample as a stand-in for the whole population:

  1. Draw 8 scores from your 8 with replacement (some get picked twice, some skipped).
  2. Take the mean of that resample.
  3. Repeat thousands of times (say 10,000).
  4. Keep the middle 95% of those means (2.5th to 97.5th percentile).

Here that gives roughly 0.65 to 0.74, reported as 0.70 [0.65, 0.74].

Comparing two systems: bootstrap the difference

Do not compute two intervals and eyeball the overlap. Subtract the two systems’ scores per input first and bootstrap those differences, which cancels out the noise from some inputs being harder than others.

Fine-tuned minus prompted on 10 images: +0.02, −0.01, +0.04, 0.00, −0.01, +0.03, +0.02, +0.01, −0.01, +0.04. The mean gain is +0.013 and the bootstrap 95% interval is [+0.001, +0.025]. The whole interval is above zero, so the fine-tuned model probably has a slight edge, though a wide interval relative to the gain says more images would help. If it had crossed zero, the honest conclusion would be “no clear difference.”

4. Human Evaluation

Automatic metrics are proxies for human perception, so for style, mood or aesthetics you ask people, and design the study so you do not nudge them:

  • Blind and randomize: raters should not know which model made which output, and both the item order and the side each model appears on should be shuffled, since people favor the first or left option.
  • Ask one clear question: “Which caption better describes this image?” beats “Which is better?”
  • Match the format to the task: pairwise choices are fast and reliable, 1–5 scales capture nuance but people calibrate differently, and style attribution has a built-in chance level of \(\frac{1}{k}\) with \(k\) choices.
  • Put error bars on human scores too, using the same bootstrap.

Do the raters agree?

Two people guessing at random still agree some of the time, so raw agreement is misleading. Cohen’s kappa corrects for that:

\[ \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} \]

Here \(p_o\) is the fraction of items where the raters actually agreed and \(p_e\) is the agreement you would expect from chance given each rater’s own yes/no habits. \(\kappa = 1\) is perfect agreement and \(\kappa = 0\) is chance level. Use Fleiss’ kappa or Krippendorff’s alpha for three or more raters.

Worked example: calculating agreement. Two raters evaluate 50 images, answering "Is this image in the target style?"

They agree on \(20 + 15 = 35\) out of 50 images, so raw agreement is \(p_o = \frac{35}{50} = 0.70\). However, Rater 1 says "yes" 50% of the time (0.50) and Rater 2 says "yes" 60% of the time (0.60). By pure chance, they would both say "yes" \(0.50 \times 0.60 = 0.30\) of the time, and both say "no" \(0.50 \times 0.40 = 0.20\) of the time, giving a chance agreement rate of \(p_e = 0.30 + 0.20 = 0.50\).

Plugging those in gives \(\kappa = \frac{0.70 - 0.50}{1 - 0.50} = 0.40\). While "70% agreement" sounds impressive on paper, subtracting chance reveals only fair-to-moderate consistency. Providing clearer definitions or reference examples would likely sharpen that score.

When metrics and people disagree

A captioner that lists every object can earn a great CLIPScore while readers find it robotic, and a music model can post a good FAD while listeners hear glitches. Neither side is automatically right. Look at the examples where they clash: the metric usually measures something real, just a narrower slice of quality than people care about.

5. Failures and Reproducibility

Bad outputs are diagnostic data if you treat each interesting one like a small experiment:

  1. Document it: log the exact input, output, seed and checkpoint so anyone can reproduce it.
  2. Form one hypothesis: for example, “the model ignores exact counts.”
  3. Run the smallest test that could prove you wrong: generate 20 images each for “one apple,” “two apples” and “three apples” with fixed seeds and count. If the counts barely change, the hypothesis holds. If instead you suspect your training captions rarely had numbers, search them for number words. An untested hypothesis is just a story.

For every run, log the configuration, seeds, exact model checkpoint (a file hash is best), hardware and runtime, and keep failed runs too. One command should regenerate every number in your results table from the logs. Finally, rerun your key evaluation on fresh inputs you have never looked at, which is the strongest proof the gain is real.

Check Your Understanding

Question 1: A tempting comparison

A teammate says their fine-tuned image model “clearly beats” the base model, showing 5 hand-picked outputs from each, made with different prompts. What is wrong, and how do you fix it?

Show answer

Five hand-picked outputs show best cases, and different prompts change two variables at once. Generate from both models on the same held-out prompts, seeds and settings, score the whole batch, and report the average with a bootstrap confidence interval.

Question 2: Reading an interval

The 95% bootstrap interval for “fine-tuned minus prompted” is [−0.02, +0.05]. What can you conclude?

Show answer

It crosses zero, so the data cannot confirm a real difference: the fine-tuned model could be slightly better or slightly worse. More evaluation examples would narrow the range.

Key Takeaways

  • Compare fairly: same inputs, seeds and settings, against a strong prompted baseline, with a prediction written down first.
  • Use ablations to find bottlenecks, keeping in mind that steps interact.
  • Quantify uncertainty: bootstrap 95% intervals on averages, and bootstrap the per-input differences when comparing two models.
  • Keep human studies honest: blind and randomize, check rater agreement with kappa, and compare against chance.
  • Turn failures into tested hypotheses, log everything, and confirm on fresh data.
