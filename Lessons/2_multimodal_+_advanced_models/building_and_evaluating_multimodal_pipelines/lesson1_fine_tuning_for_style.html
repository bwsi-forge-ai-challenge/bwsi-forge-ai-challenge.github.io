Fine-Tuning for a Style

Pretrained models write, draw and compose in a fairly generic voice. To get a specific style you have to push them off that default. This lesson covers whether fine-tuning is worth it, which parts to train, and why the dataset matters more than the training settings.

Before we dive in, it helps to be familiar with:

  • LoRA, DreamBooth and caption dropout (Generating Images by Removing Noise, Section 5). LoRA is explained again below.
  • The encoder, connector and language model recipe (Teaching a Language Model to See)
  • Music captioners and codec language models (Recipes vs. Records to Music Captioning)
  • Shared embedding spaces and cosine similarity (Representation & Latent Spaces)

1. Try Prompting First

Fine-tuning costs GPU hours, data collection and debugging. Before paying that, see how far a good prompt gets you: “a watercolor painting, faded colors, soft edges” might already be 90% of the way there. Your fine-tuned model has to beat that, not a lazy prompt on the raw model. So compare three variants on the same held-out inputs:

VariantWhat it isWhat it tells you
Zero-shotThe base model with a plain instructionWhere you start
PromptedThe base model with your best style promptWhat you get without training
Fine-tunedThe model trained on your datasetWhat training adds on top of a good prompt

Some styles resist prompting: the model barely saw them in pretraining, the words are too vague (“cozy”, “gritty”), or the style needs strict constraints like a fixed caption length. Fine-tuning pays off there. If it loses to a good prompt, that is still a useful result worth documenting.

2. LoRA: Train a Small Add-On

Fine-tuning every weight of a model with hundreds of millions of parameters is slow, and it leaves you with a full copy of the model for every style. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) avoids both. It freezes the original weights and trains a small add-on next to them.

Because the adapter’s output is added to what the frozen weights produce, the original path stays exactly as it was and the adapter only has to learn the change you want. Remove the adapter and you have the original model back. It is usually attached to the attention layers, and the same trick works on any transformer, not just diffusion models.

Why the add-on is small

Take a weight matrix that maps 1000 inputs to 1000 outputs. A full update to it has 1,000,000 numbers. LoRA instead trains two thin matrices, one of size 1000 × r and one of size r × 1000, and multiplies them to get an update of the full size. The small number r is the adapter’s rank: how wide its narrow middle is.

The rank sets how much change the adapter can express. A low rank is the smallest and safest but may be too limited to capture your style, so the output looks like the base model. A high rank has room for more detail, but the file is bigger and the adapter can start memorizing a small dataset.

3. Which Parts to Train

Most generative models have two kinds of parts: translators that convert between raw signals and internal representations (vision encoders, text encoders, audio codecs, image decoders), and a generator that decides what to produce. The translators are already well tuned and easy to break, and style lives almost entirely in the generator. So freeze the translators and put LoRA on the generator.

  • Image captioners (Vision-Language Models): The vision encoder already knows how to turn pixels into useful visual features. Your style lives in the phrasing and word choice, so place the LoRA adapters on the language model’s attention layers. Training the vision encoder on a small style dataset usually just makes it worse at seeing.
  • Music captioners: The same logic, with an audio encoder in place of the vision encoder. Because your style is defined by the vocabulary of the captions, the language decoder is where the learning needs to happen.
  • Text-to-music (Codec Language Models): The audio codec turns tokens back into sound, while the transformer decoder decides which tokens to write. Adapting the transformer changes the musical composition and style, while keeping the codec frozen keeps your audio fidelity from falling apart.
  • Text-to-image (Diffusion): The denoiser determines what the image looks like, so that is where your adapters go. The text encoder and image decoder stay frozen.

Treat these as starting defaults rather than unbreakable laws. If your target style relies on a nuance that a frozen component cannot represent (say, a rare instrument that the audio codec mangles), you may have to unfreeze and adapt more of the pipeline. Just make sure you have a clear reason why.

Three settings to watch

  • Rank: too low and the output looks like the base model; too high and the adapter can memorize a small dataset.
  • Learning rate: too low and nothing changes; too high and the loss bounces and outputs collapse into noise or one repeated pattern.
  • Training steps: too few and the style is faint; too many and the model repeats its training examples and forgets general concepts.

Training loss will not tell you when to stop. Save checkpoints and look at (or listen to) what they produce on inputs they have never seen.

4. The Dataset Is the Style

LoRA learns whatever your examples have in common. A clean, cohesive dataset gives you the style; a messy one gives you a muddy average. You rarely have thousands of perfect examples, so the usual route is to take a large public dataset and filter it:

  • Metadata: tags, genres, keywords, existing captions. Fast, but user tags are often incomplete or wrong.
  • Embedding similarity: embed a few “gold standard” examples and keep items that land nearby. CLIP does this for images and text, CLAP for audio and text.
  • A small classifier: hand-label a few hundred items as in-style or not, train a classifier on their embeddings, and let it filter the rest.

Choosing a similarity threshold

With embedding similarity, you keep every item whose cosine similarity to the average of your reference examples is above a threshold τ.

Good and bad items always overlap, so a higher τ gives a smaller, cleaner set but throws away some good examples, and a lower τ keeps more but lets noise through. No formula picks τ for you. Look at the data: sample some kept items and some rejected ones, and adjust. Write down every rule you apply and how many items it removed.

Keep a clean test set

Carve out the held-out set before you train, and do not peek until final evaluation. Data leakage is when near-copies end up on both sides of the split: the same photo at two crops, two clips cut from one song, several captions of one image. Split by source (song, original photo, album), not by slice or caption, and check across the split for near-duplicates with embedding similarity. Keep synthetic data (such as LLM-rewritten captions) out of the test set, and say when you used it.

Document it

Every dataset should come with a datasheet (Gebru et al., 2018): where the data came from, its licenses, which filters you applied and how much each removed, and what is over- or under-represented. Check licenses carefully: CC0 and public domain are free to use, Creative Commons licenses may require attribution (BY) or forbid commercial use (NC) or derivatives (ND), link-only datasets cannot be redistributed, and model weights have licenses too. Fine-tuning to imitate one living artist copies them without consent, so aim for a broad shared style such as an era, a genre or a medium.

Check Your Understanding

Question 1: A disappointing result

Your fine-tuned captioner easily beats the zero-shot model on a style classifier, but scores about the same as the prompted model. What are two possible explanations, and how could you tell them apart?

Show answer

Either a good prompt already captures the style, so fine-tuning was not needed, or your training data was too noisy or small to teach anything beyond the prompt. Inspect a random sample of the training data for mess, then retrain on a cleaner subset or test both models on cases where prompting fails.

Question 2: A suspicious score

A teammate’s music model scores far better on their held-out set than on brand-new prompts. What should you check first?

Show answer

Data leakage. Check whether held-out clips were cut from the same songs as training clips, and scan the split for pairs with very high embedding similarity.

Key Takeaways

  • Beat a good prompt, not a lazy one: compare zero-shot, prompted and fine-tuned on the same held-out inputs.
  • Freeze the translators, adapt the generator: and judge checkpoints on unseen inputs rather than the loss curve.
  • Curate ruthlessly: filter with metadata, CLIP/CLAP similarity or a small classifier, and inspect what you keep and what you reject.
  • Protect the test set and document the data: split by source, and record sources, licenses and filters in a datasheet.
