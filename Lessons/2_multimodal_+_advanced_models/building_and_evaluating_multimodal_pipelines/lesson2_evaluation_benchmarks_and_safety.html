Evaluation, Benchmarks, and Safety

A model can ace a visual benchmark without really looking at the image, because language models are so good at guessing. This lesson covers how to measure what survives each step of a pipeline, how meaning drifts over repeated cycles, what the major benchmarks test, how multimodal systems fail, and how to prove a model is using its input.

1. Why Multimodal Evaluation Is Hard

  • Many answers can be right. One photo has countless valid descriptions, so penalizing a caption for not matching one reference word for word is unfair (see Music Captioning).
  • Questions leak their answers. “What color is the banana?” is almost always “yellow”, so a language model can score points from the text alone.
  • Test data sneaks into training. Models are trained on web scrapes that often contain benchmark questions, so a high score may be memorization.
  • More modalities mean more places to fail. A wrong answer could be a perception, alignment or reasoning error, and one accuracy number will not say which.

2. Measuring What Survives a Pipeline

In a cascaded pipeline, one model’s output is the next model’s input, like a loop that describes an image in words, composes music from the words, captions the music, and draws a new image. Each time information crosses a modality, two things happen: details get dropped (music has no direct way to say “a red frisbee”) and details get invented (a model fills gaps with whatever is most common in its training data, and the next model treats that as fact). Over several hops, specifics vanish and clichés take their place.

Errors compound

If every step keeps 90% of the meaning, \(n\) steps keep about \(0.9^{n}\): 90% after one, 73% after three, 59% after five. Steps are not equal in practice, since crossing a modality loses far more than staying inside one, and the weakest link dominates. Measure it first.

Check your understanding: compounding loss

Four steps retain 70% (image to caption), 40% (caption to music), 60% (music to caption) and 80% (caption to image). How much survives one pass, and where is most of it lost?

Show answer

\(0.70 \times 0.40 \times 0.60 \times 0.80\) is about 13%. The biggest drop is caption to music, because turning concrete visual nouns into sound is inherently lossy.

Fidelity and style

If the pipeline should also have a personality, ask two separate questions at every step: fidelity (did the content survive?) and style (does it look, read or sound the way it should?). They often pull against each other, since a flashy caption drops facts and a literal one loses its voice, so measure both.

Fidelity metrics

Word-for-word matching fails on creative outputs, so we use embedding spaces and targeted checks:

  • Text similarity: “A puppy on the sand” and “A small dog on a beach” share almost no words but mean the same thing. A sentence embedding model maps both to vectors, and cosine similarity measures closeness in meaning regardless of phrasing.
  • CLIP similarity: CLIP embeds images and text into one shared space. Image–image similarity tracks how far a generated picture has drifted from an original, and image–text similarity scores how well a caption fits a photo. CLIPScore is a standard version of this; compare scores computed with the same library.
  • CLAP similarity: CLAP does for audio what CLIP does for images, scoring audio–text and audio–audio similarity.
  • Attribute checks: embeddings are blurry. If a prompt says “slow, solo piano,” check those traits directly with a beat tracker, an instrument tagger, or a human checklist.
  • Set-level metrics: FID (images) and FAD (audio) compare the average and spread of embeddings of a generated set against a real set. Lower means closer to real. They need a large batch and cannot tell you which individual outputs are good.

Style metrics

  • Similarity to a style set: the average embedding similarity between your outputs and a held-out collection in the target style.
  • A style classifier: train a small classifier to tell your styled outputs from the base model’s. If it spots the difference easily, the style is present; if it hovers near 50%, the two are indistinguishable.
  • Human raters: ask people which style an output belongs to. This is the gold standard that automatic metrics approximate.

Metrics live on different scales: a CLIP image–text score near 0.30 is often a good match, while image–image scores for related pictures run much higher, so never compare numbers across metric types. Embeddings are also blurry: two images can have nearly identical embeddings and still differ in the one detail you care about, which is why attribute checks help.

3. Drift Over Many Cycles

Feed a pipeline’s output back in as the next input and plot each cycle’s similarity to the original. This is a drift curve.

Curves usually drop sharply on the first cycle and then flatten, because the loop settles into an attractor: a stable rut the models keep generating no matter what came before. It is often the statistical cliché of the training data (a generic sunset, an upbeat pop track) or a caricature of your fine-tuned style. What a loop converges to often tells you more about a model’s biases than a table of numbers.

A single similarity score hides what was lost, so tag concepts in your starting inputs and check whether each survives every hop. Literal concepts (a red bicycle, two people) usually die when a modality cannot represent them. Mood concepts (lonely, playful, tense) can often cross modalities intact. Checking by hand is the most reliable; if you use an automatic detector, spot-check it against human labels first.

4. Benchmarks and Failure Modes

Model cards boast scores on a familiar set of benchmarks, each testing something different:

BenchmarkModalitiesWhat it testsFormat and metric
MME (2023)Image + text14 subtasks covering perception (existence, counting, position, color, OCR) and cognition (commonsense, math, code)Yes/no questions (two per image); accuracy
MMMU (2023)Image + textCollege-level reasoning: 11,500 exam and textbook questions across 30 subjects with charts, diagrams, and moleculesMultiple choice and short answer; accuracy
SEED-Bench (2023)Image, video + text19,000 questions across 12 dimensions, including scene understanding, spatial relations, and video actionsMultiple choice; accuracy
AudioCaps (2019)Audio + textDescribing everyday sounds across ~46,000 ten-second clips with human captionsCaptioning and retrieval; similarity scores and recall
LibriSpeech (2015)Speech + text~1,000 hours of read English audiobooks split into "clean" and noisier "other" test setsSpeech recognition; word error rate (WER)

How multimodal systems fail

  • Cross-modal hallucination: describing things that are not there, like a fork next to a cake because they co-occur in training. CHAIR counts hallucinated objects in captions, and POPE asks “Is there a ___ in the image?” about absent objects.
  • Visual prompt injection: models read text inside images like typed prompts. A handwritten “iPod” note on an apple fooled CLIP into calling it an iPod (a typographic attack), and hidden instructions in an image can override a chatbot’s rules.
  • Audio adversarial attacks: nearly inaudible noise can make a speech recognizer transcribe any command an attacker chooses, and ultrasonic commands can be inaudible to people entirely.
  • Modality collapse: if most questions can be answered from the text, the model stops looking at the image or audio. The original VQA dataset had this problem, so VQA v2 paired each question with two similar images that have different answers.

What to do about it

  • Treat every input as untrusted: text pulled from an image, PDF or transcript should never be run as instructions.
  • Red-team every modality, not just text: typographic attacks, hidden low-contrast text, noisy audio, mismatched media pairs.
  • Track hallucination separately from accuracy, with checks like POPE on your own data.
  • Let the model say “I can’t tell,” keep a human in the loop for high-stakes use, and decide before launch what styles you will refuse to imitate.

5. Does the Model Actually Use the Input?

High accuracy can hide a model that is guessing from text, so run the same questions under four conditions that strip away or alter the evidence. Music Captioning’s “Ignoring the audio” check (audio Lesson 4) is one example of this.

ConditionWhat you give the modelWhat a grounded model should do
FullThe real image or audio plus the questionAnswer correctly
BlindThe question alone (or with a blank image or silence)Drop sharply, or say it cannot tell
MismatchedThe question with an image or clip from a different exampleChange its answer
CounterfactualA minimal edit that flips the answer (recolor a car, swap a bark for a siren)Switch to the new correct answer
  • Reliance gap \(= \text{Accuracy (Full)} - \text{Accuracy (Blind)}\). A tiny gap means the input barely matters.
  • Change rate: the share of answers that change under Mismatched. A low rate means the model ignores what it is shown.
  • Flip rate: the share of Counterfactual edits where the model switches to the new right answer. This is the sharpest test of grounding.

Example. Two vision models on the same 200 questions:

ModelFullBlindReliance gapChange rateFlip rate
Model A82%79%3 points12%15%
Model B78%41%37 points88%71%

A leaderboard picks Model A, but it scores 79% blindfolded and barely changes with the photo, so it is mostly guessing from language. Model B looks at the picture.

Write questions whose answers depend on the media, keep counterfactual edits to one detail, and fix your questions and scoring before looking at outputs.

A 4-point reliance gap and a 9% change rate mean the model is barely listening and answers mostly from the question text. Build counterfactual clips (same scene, one key sound swapped) to measure the flip rate, and check whether your questions can be answered without the audio.

Key Takeaways

  • Pipelines lose and invent information, and losses compound across steps.
  • Measure fidelity and style separately: sentence embeddings, CLIP and CLAP for single items, FID/FAD for sets, and classifiers or people for style.
  • Loops drift toward attractors, so track what survives, literal objects versus mood.
  • Benchmark scores can hide guessing: test with full, blind, mismatched and counterfactual inputs, and treat all inputs as untrusted.
