Music Captioning: From Sound Back to Words

The audio deep dive went from text to sound. This lesson goes the other way: a model listens to a clip and writes a description of it. Music captioners use the same recipe as the image captioners from the vision unit, with an audio encoder in place of the vision encoder, but they have their own problems, mostly about where the training captions come from and what a caption can honestly claim.

1. Running Text-to-Music Backwards

In a text-to-music model, the text is the condition and audio tokens are the output. A music captioner swaps the two. The audio is the condition, and the model predicts a caption one word at a time:

\[ P(w_1, \dots, w_N \mid \text{audio}) = \prod_{t=1}^{N} P(w_t \mid w_{<t}, \text{audio}) \]

This is the same next-token prediction a language model does, with one extra input. Training uses cross-entropy on the caption tokens. During training the decoder is shown the true previous words of the caption (called teacher forcing), so every position can be scored in one pass. At generation time it has to use its own previous words instead.

Two things make captioning music harder than it looks:

  • There is no single right answer. "Mellow late-night jazz with brushed drums" and "A slow trio piece, piano and upright bass, quiet and warm" can both be correct for the same clip. Scoring against one reference caption punishes good descriptions that use different words.
  • Paired data is scarce. There are millions of captioned images but only a few thousand music clips with sentences written by people. Most music datasets have tags (genre, mood, instrument) instead of sentences. Section 4 is about what to do with that.

2. The Recipe: Encoder, Projection, Decoder

Most music captioners have three parts:

  1. An audio encoder turns the clip into a sequence of feature vectors, usually one per short slice of time. It is pretrained and usually frozen.
  2. A projection (a linear layer or a small MLP) maps those vectors into the size the language model expects. It often also pools neighbouring frames together so there are fewer of them. This part is small and trained from scratch.
  3. A text decoder (a pretrained language model) reads the projected audio vectors and writes the caption. It is fine-tuned, fully or with LoRA.

Pooling is a trade-off. Fewer audio tokens make training faster and force the projection to summarize, but a change that lasts less than one pooled window (a short drum fill, a single sung word) can disappear.

Prefix or cross-attention?

The first figure in this section shows the prefix approach: audio tokens are placed in front of the words, in the same sequence. The other option is cross-attention, exactly as in Steering the AI but with the roles reversed: the caption tokens are the queries and the audio features supply the keys and values. Prefixing is easier to bolt onto an existing language model because it adds no new layers. Cross-attention adds layers that start untrained, but it handles long audio sequences without making the text sequence longer. Both are forms of the intermediate fusion you saw in Fusion Strategies.

3. Which Audio Encoder?

The encoder decides what the decoder can possibly "hear." Two widely used families learn very different things:

  • CLAP's audio encoder was trained contrastively on audio and caption pairs, the audio version of CLIP. Its features already line up with language, so a small projection can connect them to a decoder. It is trained to summarize a whole clip as one vector, so it is good at overall genre, mood and instruments and weaker at what happens when.
  • MERT was trained only on music, with no text at all. It learns by predicting hidden parts of a clip, using targets built from codec tokens (like those in The Sound Dictionary) and a pitch-based spectrogram. Its frame-level features carry detail about pitch, rhythm and timbre, but they say nothing about words, so the projection and decoder have to learn the whole mapping to language from your paired data.

A complete pretrained captioner (for example, one released with a music captioning paper) bundles an encoder and decoder that were already trained together. Starting from one is the fastest path to a working model. Building your own from an encoder and a decoder gives you more control over each part, at the cost of more training.

4. Where Captions Come From

The decoder learns its vocabulary and sentence shapes entirely from the text side of the training data. Whatever voice the captions have, the model will have. So the question "where do my captions come from?" decides both how accurate the model is and how it sounds.

SourceExampleStrengthRisk
Captions written by peopleMusicCaps (about 5,500 clips described by musicians), Song Describer (about 1,100 captions)Accurate and variedSmall; may not match your vocabulary
Tags filled into templatesTags from MTG-Jamendo or FMA turned into sentences by rules you writeLarge, and only says what the tags sayEvery caption has the same few shapes
Tags rewritten by a language modelLP-MusicCaps (about 2.2 million captions generated from tags)Large and fluentCan invent details the tags never mentioned
Prompts of generated musicClips from a text-to-music model, paired with the prompt that made themUnlimited and on-styleThe prompt says what was asked for, not what came out

The last row is the easiest to get wrong. If a text-to-music model was asked for "harp and strings" but produced only strings, the pair teaches the captioner to say "harp" when there is none. A captioner trained this way also learns the habits of that one generator rather than how music sounds in general.

Generated or rewritten captions are synthetic data. A common pattern is to mix real and synthetic pairs and treat the mix ratio as something you test, not assume. Whatever the mix, evaluate on captions written by people that the model never trained on, or you will be measuring how well it copies the synthetic source.

    What did the activity show?

    Every caption is accurate, because templates only repeat the tags. But six captions use at most four sentence shapes, and a model trained on thousands of them will write in those four shapes forever. More templates help a little; human-written or rewritten captions help more.

    The augmentations show a second point. Speeding a clip up by 1.25× changes its tempo, so the BPM tag has to change with it, and the tempo word may change too. Shifting up 2 semitones moves the key. If you augment the audio but keep the old tags, you have created training pairs whose captions are wrong.

    5. Augmenting Audio Without Lying to the Labels

    Augmentation makes new training examples by changing existing ones, which is useful when paired data is scarce. For audio-text pairs (whether you are training a captioner or a text-to-music model), the rule is simple: an augmentation is safe only if every claim in the caption is still true afterwards. If a claim changes, either update the caption to match or don't use that augmentation.

    AugmentationWhat it changesCaption claims it can break
    Random crop or offsetWhich part of the song you hear"Starts with a drum intro," "builds to a loud ending"
    Gain change, added noise, reverbLoudness and recording quality"Clean studio sound," "intimate and dry"
    Time stretchTempo (and feel, if large)Any BPM or tempo word; possibly mood
    Pitch shiftKey; large shifts also change timbreThe key; "deep male vocal"; instrument identity
    Mixing stems or two clipsWhich instruments are presentThe instrument list; "solo piano"

    Small, label-preserving changes (a short crop, a little gain or noise) are usually safe for captions about genre and mood. Changes to tempo and pitch are safe only when you update the caption with them. Mixing is the most powerful and the most dangerous: it can teach a model about combinations it has rarely heard, but only if the caption lists every instrument in the mix.

    6. What Music Captioners Get Wrong

    • Falling back on common words. If most training captions mention drums, the model will mention drums for a solo cello. Compare how often each word appears in the model's captions with how often it appears in human references.
    • Confident but wrong numbers. Tempo estimates are often off by exactly half or double, because a beat at 70 BPM and one at 140 BPM can sound like the same pulse. A stated BPM is worth checking against a beat tracker.
    • Vague moods. "Relaxing" fits almost any slow clip. Mood words that fit everything carry no information, even when they are technically correct.
    • Ignoring the audio. A decoder can learn to write a plausible caption from its language knowledge alone. Feed it silence, white noise or two very different clips: if the captions barely change, the audio isn't getting through. Possible causes include a projection that never trained, a bug in how features are passed, or training captions so uniform that the audio never mattered.

    Check Your Understanding

    Question 1: a stretched clip

    Question: A clip is tagged "90 BPM, relaxed, A minor." You time-stretch it to play 1.5× faster. Which tags can you keep?

    Answer: The key stays A minor, because time stretching keeps pitch. The tempo becomes 135 BPM. "Relaxed" may no longer be true at 135 BPM, so either listen and relabel the mood or use a smaller stretch where the mood clearly survives.

    Question 2: the same caption every time

    Question: Your captioner writes nearly the same sentence for a jazz ballad and a techno track. Give two possible causes and a quick test for each.

    Answer: The audio may not be reaching the decoder (an untrained or disconnected projection): test by feeding silence and real clips and comparing the outputs. Or the training captions may be too uniform, for example from only a few templates: test by counting distinct sentences or opening words in the training set.

    Question 3: choosing an encoder

    Question: Why does a captioner built on MERT features usually need more paired data than one built on CLAP features?

    Answer: CLAP's features were trained to line up with text, so the projection only has to make a small adjustment. MERT's features know a lot about music but nothing about language, so the projection and decoder have to learn the whole mapping from sound to words from your paired examples.

    Key Takeaways

    • A music captioner is next-token prediction conditioned on audio: a frozen audio encoder, a small trained projection and a fine-tuned text decoder.
    • The encoder sets what the model can hear. Text-aligned encoders (CLAP) need less paired data; music-only encoders (MERT) keep more musical detail.
    • The captions set how the model talks. Templates are accurate but repetitive, rewritten captions are fluent but can invent details, and generator prompts describe what was asked for, not what came out.
    • Evaluate on human-written captions the model never trained on.
    • An augmentation is safe only if the caption stays true. Update tempo, key and instrument claims when the audio changes.
    • Check that the audio matters: captions for silence and for real music should differ.
