Multimodal & Advanced Models · Project 1

Project 1 Capstone: An End-to-End Multimodal Assistant

With a partner, you will build a lightweight assistant that takes a photo, an audio clip, and a question, and answers in text that is grounded in what it actually saw and heard. Then you will measure it the way a real team would: accuracy, alignment, generalization, grounding, and speed.

Weight: 20% of your final grade
Team size: 2 students
Length: about 3 weeks, with three Gradescope submissions
Environment: Google Colab with a free T4 GPU, using open models from Hugging Face

This project ties the module together. Part 1 gave you representations, alignment, and fusion; the deep dives showed how audio and image models work inside; and this unit covered unified architectures and evaluation. Now you will assemble frozen pretrained models into one working system and test whether it really uses its inputs.

1. The Brief

Your assistant receives three inputs and returns one answer:

  • An image, such as a photo of a room, a street, or a desk.
  • An audio clip of up to 30 seconds, containing speech, an everyday sound, or both.
  • A text question that needs one or both of them to answer.

The answer must be grounded: it should say which input each part of it comes from, and say "I can't tell" when the evidence is not there.

Example. Image: a kitchen counter with a kettle and a toaster. Audio: a high-pitched whistle. Question: "What is making that noise?"

A good answer: "The sound is a high-pitched whistle, most likely a kettle boiling (audio). There is a kettle on the left side of the counter (image), so it is probably that kettle."

A poor answer: "It is the smoke alarm." Nothing in either input supports this.

2. The Technical Stack

Every encoder stays frozen: you use pretrained weights without retraining them. The suggested models below all fit on a Colab T4 together. You may swap any of them for another open model if you explain why in your report.

Connecting the encoders to the language model. The required baseline is a text bridge: turn every encoder's output into a structured evidence block, with labels, scores, and transcript, and put it in the language model's prompt along with the question and rules about grounding. The more ambitious option, a learned projection layer that maps CLIP embeddings straight into the language model's input space, LLaVA style, is one of the extensions below.

3. What You Will Build and Measure

Part A: Perception

Write one function per encoder that returns its evidence in a fixed format, such as a dictionary of labels and scores or a transcript with timestamps. Choose label vocabularies for CLIP and CLAP that fit your test scenes, and keep a record of every label you use.

Part B: Grounded answering

Write the prompt and the code that turn the evidence and the question into an answer. Your system must:

  • say which modality supports each claim;
  • answer "I can't tell" when no evidence passes a confidence threshold you choose and justify; and
  • never follow instructions that appear inside the image or the transcript (Lesson 2 of this unit).

Part C: Evaluation

Build a test set of at least 30 cases, each with an image, an audio clip, a question, a reference answer, and a note of which modalities the answer needs. Include cases that need only the image, only the audio, and both. Then report:

  • Answer accuracy: score every answer as correct, partly correct, or wrong against the reference, using a rubric you write before you look at the answers.
  • Cross-modal alignment accuracy: the top-1 accuracy of your CLIP labels on the images and your CLAP labels on the sounds.
  • Zero-shot generalization: after your system is finished, write at least 10 new cases with objects and sounds you never tested on during development, and compare their accuracy with the rest.
  • Grounding: run the four-condition protocol from Evaluation, Benchmarks, and Safety (full, blind, mismatched, counterfactual) on at least 20 cases, including at least 10 counterfactual pairs. Report the reliance gap, change rate, and flip rate for each modality.

Part D: Latency profiling

Time every stage (each encoder, the language model's reading of the prompt, and its generation) over your whole test set. Report the median and the 95th-percentile time for each stage and for the full answer. Then make one optimization, such as running the encoders in parallel, a smaller Whisper model, shorter prompts, or caching, and show its before-and-after effect on both speed and accuracy.

Part E: One extension

Choose one, and measure it against your baseline:

  • Learned projection adapter: train a small linear or MLP layer that maps CLIP image embeddings into the language model's input embeddings, with the language model frozen, on a small image–caption dataset. Compare it with the text bridge.
  • Hallucination check: write POPE-style "Is there a ___?" questions for your images, including objects that often appear in similar scenes but are absent, and reduce the hallucination rate.
  • Robustness: test with noisy audio, blurry or dark images, and typographic attacks, and harden the system against them.
  • Model trade-off study: compare two or three language models or Whisper sizes on accuracy, grounding, and latency.

Part F: Documentation

Submit a README that explains how to run your system, plus a 4 to 6 page report using the template in Section 6.

5. Teams, Milestones, and Submission

The capstone is done in pairs. Find a partner by replying to the pinned Project 1: Search for Teammates post on Piazza with your time zone and availability. If you have no partner by the Milestone 0 deadline, submit Milestone 0 on your own and staff will pair you within two days. If the class has an odd number of students, one team of three is formed, and that team completes two extensions instead of one.

A natural split is for one partner to own the vision side (CLIP and image tests) and the other the audio side (Whisper, CLAP, and audio tests), with both working on the language model, the evaluation, and the report. Either partner should be able to explain any part of the system.

6. Report Template (4 to 6 pages, PDF)

  1. System overview: a diagram of your pipeline and the models you chose, with reasons.
  2. Grounding design: your evidence format, prompt, confidence thresholds, and how the system abstains and resists injected instructions.
  3. Test set: how you built it, what it covers, and your scoring rubric.
  4. Results: answer accuracy, alignment accuracy, zero-shot generalization, and the grounding protocol, in tables or plots.
  5. Latency: the per-stage profile and your optimization.
  6. Extension: what you tried and how it compared with the baseline.
  7. Failure analysis: at least three failures, with the inputs, the answer, and which stage caused each.
  8. Contribution and AI-use statements: who did what, and how you used AI tools.

7. Rules and Tips

  • Models: any open model from Hugging Face is allowed. Paid APIs are not required; if you use one for comparison, report its cost.
  • AI assistants: allowed for writing and debugging code, following the Generative AI Use Policy. Your report's analysis must be your own writing.
  • Data: use your own photos and recordings or openly licensed ones, and credit everything you did not create. Do not use photos or voice recordings of people without their permission, and keep all content appropriate for class.
  • Compute: load each model once and reuse it, use the GPU runtime, and test on one case before running everything. Save results to Google Drive as you go, because Colab sessions reset.
  • Start small: get one case working end to end with the simplest version of every part before improving anything.
