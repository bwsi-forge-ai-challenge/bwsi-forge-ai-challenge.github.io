In Lesson 1 you learned that a generator can map a compact latent z to a full pixel grid. But who decides whether the output looks real? In a GAN — a Generative Adversarial Network — that job goes to a second network trained as an adversary. Two players, one game, no labeled “correct answer” image required.

Building on what you know. The Generator is the upsampler you met last lesson: z → image. The Discriminator is a binary classifier from your ML prereqs — same family as “cat vs dog,” but now the classes are real photo vs fake. Training is a tug-of-war: each network’s loss pushes against the other’s.

1. Two roles: counterfeiter and museum curator

Metaphor: A master forger paints fake Vermeers in a attic. A museum curator (expert authenticator) learns to spot flaws. Every time the curator catches a fake, the forger studies the rejection and tries again. Every time a fake slips through, the curator sharpens their eye. Neither can win forever — they co-evolve.
RoleNetworkInput → OutputGoal
Forger Generator G Random noise z → fake image G(z) Make fakes that look real enough to fool the curator
Detective Discriminator D Any image → score in [0, 1] (“probability real”) Score real photos high, fakes low

Unlike supervised classification, you never need pairs like “this z should produce this exact cat photo.” Real images from a dataset supply the “authentic” side; the Generator manufactures the counterfeits on the fly.

sample z ~ p(z) # e.g. Gaussian vector, length 128 G(z) → fake image # Generator (Lesson 1 decoder idea) x_real ~ dataset # real photos D(x) → [0, 1] # Discriminator (binary classifier) train D on real vs fake # detective gets sharper train G to raise D(G(z)) # forger learns new tricks

Check your knowledge: which network is which?

Question: A student says, “The Discriminator generates improved images when it loses.” What is wrong with that statement?

Show answer

Only the Generator creates new images. The Discriminator never outputs pixels — it outputs a score (real vs fake). When D “loses,” that means G produced a fake D couldn’t reject; G gets the gradient signal to keep fooling D, not D itself painting pictures.

2. The min–max game in plain language

Think of a single number line measuring “how well the detective is doing.” The Discriminator wants to push that number up by confidently labeling reals as real and fakes as fake. The Generator wants to push the same setup so the detective is wrong on fakes — effectively minimizing the detective’s success on counterfeits.

minG maxD   V(D, G) = Ex~real[log D(x)] + Ez[log(1 − D(G(z)))]
Worked intuition (no calculus required).
  • D’s turn (maximize): On a real photo, D should output near 1 → log D(x) is large (less negative). On a fake, D should output near 0 → log(1 − D(G(z))) is also large.
  • G’s turn (minimize): G wants D(G(z)) close to 1 even though the image is fake — so the second term log(1 − D(G(z))) blows up negative and G’s loss pushes G to improve.
  • Equilibrium fantasy: At the Nash ideal, G’s fakes sit on the data manifold and D can only guess 50/50 — like a curator who truly cannot tell.

Training alternates (or interleaves) updates — not unlike how you saw separate forward/backward passes in the training-loop prereq, except here two models take turns on opposing objectives.

Check your knowledge: log trick

Question: If D correctly outputs 0.01 on a fake (good for D), what happens to log(1 − 0.01) ≈ log(0.99)? Is that good or bad for D’s objective?

Show answer

log(0.99) ≈ −0.01 — close to zero, which is good for D (D wants to maximize, so values near 0 beat large negatives like log(0.5) ≈ −0.69). If D wrongly said 0.99 on a fake, log(1 − 0.99) = log(0.01) ≈ −4.6 — terrible for D, great signal for G.

3. Why the tug-of-war is fragile

GAN training is famous for being finicky. If the Discriminator learns too fast, it always rejects fakes with near-certainty — gradients to the Generator vanish (“G gets no useful feedback”). If the Generator learns too fast, it may find one weird image that always fools D while ignoring diversity — mode collapse (every z maps to the same face).

  • D too strong: G’s loss flatlines; samples stay noisy or frozen.
  • G too strong / collapsed: D is confused but outputs look identical or surreal.
  • Balanced: Fakes gradually sharpen; D accuracy hovers below perfection.

Learning rates, architecture, and tricks (label smoothing, spectral norm, Wasserstein variants) exist because of this balance problem — but the core story remains: two networks, opposing losses, shared data stream.

Check your knowledge: mode collapse

Question: You sample 100 different noise vectors z but G outputs nearly the same portrait every time. D’s accuracy on fakes is ~50%. Is training “solved”?

Show answer

No — that is classic mode collapse. D may be fooled by one trick image, but G failed to cover the full diversity of faces. A healthy GAN should produce varied samples across different z while still looking realistic.

5. From toy GANs to StyleGAN faces

Early GAN outputs were blurry 64×64 thumbnails. StyleGAN (2019) stacked progressive growing, style mixing, and a carefully balanced discriminator to reach photorealistic faces at 1024×1024. The latent walk you previewed in Lesson 1 — nearby z vectors morph smoothly — comes from this line of work.

  • This Person Does Not Exist — each refresh samples a StyleGAN2 face (notice lighting, pose, and identity change).
