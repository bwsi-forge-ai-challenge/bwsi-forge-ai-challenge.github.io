Generating Images by Removing Noise: Diffusion

Why generate images by removing noise?

Making a brand-new image in one go is hard. A network would have to turn random numbers into a finished picture in a single pass, getting the layout, the objects and every fine detail right all at once.

Diffusion splits that one hard job into many easy ones. Destroying an image is easy: keep adding a little random noise until only static is left, and no learning is needed. Now do it slowly, in about 1000 tiny steps. Undoing one small step is easy, because the network only has to guess what a slightly cleaner image looks like. Start from pure static, undo the steps one after another, and a picture appears.

  • Simple, stable training: we add the noise ourselves, so we know the right answer to check the network's guess against.
  • Variety: every different starting static gives a different picture.
  • Steerable: we can nudge each step, for example with a text prompt.
  • The cost: many steps means many network passes, so generation is slow. Later sections cover how to speed it up.

1. Forward diffusion: adding noise

A diffusion model is trained by first destroying images. Start with a clean image \(x_0\) and, over \(T\) steps (typically \(T = 1000\)), add a little random Gaussian noise at each step until nothing but noise is left. Picture a statue buried by a blizzard until only static remains.

\[ x_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\; x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\; \epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

Here \(x_t\) is the image after \(t\) noising steps, and \(\beta_t\) is a small number (for example between \(0.0001\) and \(0.02\)) that sets how much noise is added at step \(t\). The term \(\epsilon_t\) is fresh random noise with the same shape as the image, drawn from a standard normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\): every value is an independent draw from a bell curve with mean \(0\) and variance \(1\). The factor \(\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\) shrinks the existing image slightly, so the total amount of signal plus noise stays about the same.

The noise picture is shown at full strength, but it is multiplied by the small number \(\sqrt{\beta_t}\) before it is added, which is why the image barely changes in one step. Here \(\beta_t\) grows from \(0.0001\) at \(t = 1\) to \(0.02\) at \(t = 1000\).

You do not need to run the steps one by one. Define \(\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t\) and let \(\bar{\alpha}_t = \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \cdots \alpha_t\) be the product of all of them so far. Then you can jump straight to any step \(t\):

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\; x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\; \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

This is a strong claim: the noise from \(t\) separate steps has collapsed into one noise term. You can prove it yourself by induction on \(t\). Try it as a challenge.

So \(x_t\) is a weighted mix of the original image and one batch of noise. At \(t = 0\), \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) is close to \(1\) and the image is almost untouched. As \(t\) grows, \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) falls toward \(0\) and the image is replaced by noise.

Worked example. Suppose \(\bar{\alpha}_t = 0.25\) at some step. Then \(x_t = \sqrt{0.25}\, x_0 + \sqrt{0.75}\, \epsilon = 0.5\, x_0 + 0.87\, \epsilon\). The result is half image and a bit more than half noise, and the two squared weights add up to \(0.25 + 0.75 = 1\).

2. Training: learning to predict the noise

The model is a neural network written \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\). This network's job is to guess the noise \(\epsilon\) that was mixed into the image. The subscript \(\theta\) stands for the network's weights, which are what training adjusts, and the inputs in brackets are the noisy image \(x_t\) and the step number \(t\) (so it knows how noisy the input is). Written out: the noise \(\epsilon\), as estimated by a network with weights \(\theta\), given \(x_t\) and \(t\). The true noise is \(\epsilon\); the network's guess is \(\epsilon_\theta\).

One training step is:

  1. Pick a real training image \(x_0\) and a random step \(t\) from \(1\) to \(T\).
  2. Draw noise \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and build the noisy image \(x_t\) with the shortcut formula above.
  3. Ask the network for its guess \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\).
  4. Measure how far the guess is from the true noise, and update the weights by gradient descent.
\[ \mathcal{L} = \big\lVert \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\rVert^2 \]

This is a regression problem, like predicting a number from inputs, with a mean squared error loss. No human labels are needed: the noise you added is the label. Predicting the noise is useful because if you know what the noise is, you can subtract it to get a cleaner image.

3. Sampling: removing noise step by step

The noising process can be reversed. To generate a new image, start from pure noise \(x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and walk backward, one step at a time. At each step the network predicts the noise in the current image, and a small amount of it is removed. The formula for going back one step, from \(x_t\) to \(x_{t-1}\), is below. You do not need to memorize it. Just understand what it is doing:

\[ x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}}\; \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \right) \]

The pattern is current noisy image, minus a scaled prediction of its noise, repeated for \(t = T, \dots, 1\). Different runs give different images because each one starts from different random noise.

x = random noise # x_T for t in T, T-1, ..., 1: eps = model(x, t) # predict the noise in x x = remove_some_noise(x, eps, t) return x # a new image

Making each step cheaper: shrink the image first

A \(512 \times 512\) colour image is \(786{,}432\) numbers, and running a large network on that \(1000\) times is expensive. Stable Diffusion avoids this by doing the diffusion on a much smaller version of the image. An encoder network squeezes the image down to a small grid of numbers (for example \(64 \times 64 \times 4\), about \(48\) times fewer numbers), and a decoder network can expand that small grid back into a full-size picture. All the noising and denoising happens on the small version, and at the very end the decoder turns the finished small version into the final image. The encoder and decoder are trained once and then kept frozen, and everything else in this section works the same way on the small version instead of the full image.

Squeezing an image down always throws away some information, so the decoded picture is never a perfect copy of what the small version stood for. Very fine detail, such as tiny text or small faces, is what suffers most. For image generation this is not a big problem: we are creating a new picture rather than reproducing an exact one, so a slightly softer fine detail is a price worth paying for being many times faster.

4. Steering the model with text

To draw what you ask for, the denoiser also receives the prompt. A text encoder (such as the text half of CLIP, or T5) turns the prompt into a sequence of vectors \(c\), and cross-attention layers inside the denoiser let every location in the image look up the relevant words. This happens at every denoising step, so the network becomes \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)\): the same noisy image \(x_t\) and step \(t\) as before, plus the prompt \(c\).

Classifier-free guidance: asking twice and exaggerating the difference

Feeding in the prompt is not always enough. The model may follow it only loosely and draw a vague cat. Classifier-free guidance pushes the picture harder towards the prompt. At every step the model is asked twice:

  • With the prompt: “what noise do I expect if this is a picture of a red cat on a sofa?” This answer is \(\epsilon_c\).
  • Without the prompt: “what noise do I expect for any picture at all?” This answer is \(\epsilon_\varnothing\).

The gap between the two answers, \(\epsilon_c - \epsilon_\varnothing\), points in the direction that makes the picture more like this prompt rather than just any picture. Guidance starts from the unprompted answer and moves \(w\) times that gap towards the prompt:

\[ \hat{\epsilon} = \epsilon_\varnothing + w\, (\epsilon_c - \epsilon_\varnothing) \]

The number \(w\), the guidance scale, is a dial:

  • \(w = 0\): the prompt is ignored, and \(\hat{\epsilon} = \epsilon_\varnothing\).
  • \(w = 1\): the plain prompted answer, \(\hat{\epsilon} = \epsilon_c\). No extra push.
  • \(w > 1\): the difference is exaggerated, so the image follows the prompt more strongly.

One network can give both answers because it is trained with the caption randomly blanked out some of the time. It learns to predict the noise both with and without a prompt.

5. Fine-tuning a diffusion model

To teach a diffusion model a new subject or style, you keep training it on your own images and captions, with the same noise-prediction task as before. Updating every weight of a model with hundreds of millions of parameters is heavy, so two techniques are common.

LoRA: train a small add-on

LoRA freezes the original weights, so they are never touched, and adds a small second path beside them. The same input goes down both paths:

  • The original path does exactly what it always did.
  • The adapter path first shrinks the input to a short list (say 16 numbers instead of 1024), then grows it back to full length. Its output is added to the output of the original path.

So the adapter only has to learn the change you want. Shrinking first means it has very few numbers to train, and it forces the change to be simple, which is enough to shift a style.

DreamBooth: teach a new subject from a few images

DreamBooth fine-tunes the model on a handful of images of one subject, each captioned with the same short phrase, for example “a photo of sks dog”. Each word in that phrase has a job:

  • “sks” is a blank label. It is a rare made-up word that the model has no meaning for, so training can tie your dog’s particular look (this face, this fur) to it. A real name like “Max” or an ordinary word already means something to the model, and your dog would get mixed up with that meaning.
  • “dog” says what kind of thing it is. The model already knows a great deal about dogs: their shape, poses and fur. Naming the class lets it reuse all of that and only learn what makes your dog different.

Afterwards the prompt decides what you get: “a photo of sks dog on a beach” draws your dog, and the model can place it in scenes it never saw in your photos.

The risk is that the model forgets what an ordinary dog looks like and starts drawing your dog for every dog. Prior preservation guards against this: while training on your photos, the model is also trained on ordinary pictures that the original model generates for the plain prompt “a photo of a dog”. Your photos teach it the new subject, and the ordinary pictures remind it what dogs in general look like.

Other choices that matter

  • A style token. A made-up word placed in every caption of your style set gives the model a handle to associate with that style.
  • Caption dropout. Randomly blanking some captions during fine-tuning keeps classifier-free guidance working and makes the model learn your style even without a prompt.
  • Checking for overfitting. Generate sample images at several checkpoints (copies of the model saved partway through training). A model that only reproduces its training images, or that has forgotten general concepts, has been trained too long.
