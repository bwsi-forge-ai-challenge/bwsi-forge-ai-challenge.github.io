Generating Images by Removing Noise: Diffusion

1. Forward diffusion: adding noise

A diffusion model is trained by first destroying images. Start with a clean image \(x_0\) and, over \(T\) steps (typically \(T = 1000\)), add a little random Gaussian noise at each step until nothing but noise is left. Picture a statue buried by a blizzard until only static remains.

\[ x_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\; x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\; \epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

Here \(x_t\) is the image after \(t\) noising steps, and \(\beta_t\) is a small number (for example between \(0.0001\) and \(0.02\)) that sets how much noise is added at step \(t\). The term \(\epsilon_t\) is fresh random noise with the same shape as the image, drawn from a standard normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\): every value is an independent draw from a bell curve with mean \(0\) and variance \(1\). The factor \(\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\) shrinks the existing image slightly, so the total amount of signal plus noise stays about the same.

You do not need to run the steps one by one. Define \(\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t\) and let \(\bar{\alpha}_t = \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \cdots \alpha_t\) be the product of all of them so far. Then you can jump straight to any step \(t\):

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\; x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\; \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

So \(x_t\) is a weighted mix of the original image and one batch of noise. At \(t = 0\), \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) is close to \(1\) and the image is almost untouched. As \(t\) grows, \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) falls toward \(0\) and the image is replaced by noise.

Worked example. Suppose \(\bar{\alpha}_t = 0.25\) at some step. Then \(x_t = \sqrt{0.25}\, x_0 + \sqrt{0.75}\, \epsilon = 0.5\, x_0 + 0.87\, \epsilon\). The result is half image and a bit more than half noise, and the two squared weights add up to \(0.25 + 0.75 = 1\).

2. Training: learning to predict the noise

The model is a neural network \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\), where \(\theta\) stands for its weights. It receives a noisy image and the step number \(t\) (so it knows how noisy the input is) and tries to predict the noise that was mixed in. Its architecture is usually a U-Net (a convolutional network with skip connections) or a transformer.

One training step is:

  1. Pick a real training image \(x_0\) and a random step \(t\) from \(1\) to \(T\).
  2. Draw noise \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and build the noisy image \(x_t\) with the shortcut formula above.
  3. Ask the network for its guess \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\).
  4. Measure how far the guess is from the true noise, and update the weights by gradient descent.
\[ \mathcal{L} = \big\lVert \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\rVert^2 \]

This is a regression problem, like predicting a number from inputs, with a mean squared error loss. No human labels are needed: the noise you added is the label. Predicting the noise is useful because if you know what the noise is, you can subtract it to get a cleaner image.

3. Sampling: removing noise step by step

To generate a new image, start from pure noise \(x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and walk backward. At each step the network predicts the noise in the current image, and a small amount of it is removed:

\[ x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}}\; \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \right) + \sigma_t\, z, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

Here \(\sigma_t\, z\) is a small amount of fresh random noise added back so that different runs give different images. Do not memorize the fractions. The pattern is current noisy image, minus a scaled prediction of its noise, plus a little randomness, repeated for \(t = T, \dots, 1\).

x = random noise # x_T for t in T, T-1, ..., 1: eps = model(x, t) # predict the noise in x x = remove_some_noise(x, eps, t) return x # a new image

Every step is one full pass through the network, so \(1000\) steps is slow. Faster samplers such as DDIM and DPM++ reach similar quality in about \(20\) to \(50\) steps, and distilled models such as SD-Turbo get a usable image in \(1\) to \(4\).

4. Latent diffusion: denoise small, decode big

A \(512 \times 512\) RGB image has \(512 \cdot 512 \cdot 3 = 786{,}432\) numbers, and running a large network \(1000\) times on that is expensive. Stable Diffusion avoids this by working in the compressed space of an autoencoder (a VAE). The encoder shrinks an image to a small latent of shape \(64 \times 64 \times 4\), which is \(16{,}384\) numbers, about \(48\) times fewer. The whole noising, training and sampling procedure above is then carried out on latents, and the decoder turns the final latent back into pixels.

sample z_T ~ N(0, I) shape (64, 64, 4) for t = T ... 1: z_{t-1} = denoise_step(z_t, model(z_t, t, text), t) image = VAE_decode(z_0) shape (512, 512, 3)

The autoencoder is trained once, separately, and then kept fixed. During diffusion training each image is first encoded to a latent, which plays the role of \(x_0\). Nothing else about the objective changes.

5. Steering the model with text

To draw what you ask for, the denoiser also receives the prompt. A text encoder (such as the text half of CLIP, or T5) turns the prompt into a sequence of vectors \(c\), and cross-attention layers inside the denoiser let every location in the image look up the relevant words. This happens at every denoising step, so the network becomes \(\epsilon_\theta(z_t, t, c)\).

Classifier-free guidance strengthens the effect. The model is trained with the caption randomly blanked out some of the time, so it learns both a prompted prediction \(\epsilon_c\) and an unprompted one \(\epsilon_\varnothing\). At sampling time it runs both and pushes the result away from the unprompted prediction:

\[ \hat{\epsilon} = \epsilon_\varnothing + w\, (\epsilon_c - \epsilon_\varnothing) \]

The guidance scale \(w\) is typically around \(7\) to \(8\) for Stable Diffusion 1.5. Larger \(w\) follows the prompt more closely, but too large gives oversaturated colors and distorted shapes, and it needs two network calls per step. Distilled models such as SD-Turbo are trained to work without guidance, which is why they use \(w\) off (guidance_scale=0 in the diffusers library).

6. Fine-tuning a diffusion model

To teach a diffusion model a new subject or style, you keep training it on your own images and captions with the same noise-prediction loss. Updating every weight of a model with hundreds of millions of parameters is heavy, so two techniques are common.

LoRA: train a small update

For a weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}\) in the network, LoRA freezes \(W\) and learns a low-rank update instead:

\[ W' = W + BA, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\; A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k},\; r \ll \min(d, k) \]

Only \(A\) and \(B\) are trained, which is \(r(d + k)\) numbers instead of \(dk\). For \(d = k = 1024\) and rank \(r = 16\), that is \(16 \cdot 2048 = 32{,}768\) numbers against \(1{,}048{,}576\), about \(3\%\). LoRA is usually applied to the attention layers, and the trained update is a small file that can be added to or removed from the base model.

DreamBooth: teach a new subject from a few images

DreamBooth fine-tunes the model on a handful of images of one subject, each captioned with a rare identifier word (for example "a photo of sks dog"). The risk is that the model forgets what an ordinary dog looks like. A prior-preservation loss guards against this by adding a second term, computed on images the original model generates for the plain class prompt ("a photo of a dog"):

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{subject}} + \lambda\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}} \]

Here \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{subject}}\) is the usual loss on your images, \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}\) is the same loss on the generated class images, and \(\lambda\) sets how much weight the second term gets.

Other choices that matter

  • A style token. A made-up word placed in every caption of your style set gives the model a handle to associate with that style.
  • Caption dropout. Randomly blanking some captions during fine-tuning keeps classifier-free guidance working and makes the model learn your style even without a prompt.
  • Checking for overfitting. Generate sample images at several checkpoints. A model that only reproduces its training images, or that has forgotten general concepts, has been trained too long.
