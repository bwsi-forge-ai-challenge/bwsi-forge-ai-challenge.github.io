Project 1: The Multimodal Telephone Game

In teams of 3–4, you will build a multimodal "telephone game": an image becomes a caption, the caption becomes a piece of music, the music is described back in words, and the text becomes a new image. Every team member builds and fine-tunes all four stages in their own personal style, then the team chains members' stages together and measures how meaning and style drift, mutate, and compete as the message passes from person to person.

Weight: [X]% of your final grade (instructor to set)
Team size: 3–4 students, assigned by course staff
Length: 2 weeks (14 days), followed by a live demo and oral check on Days 15–16
Deliverables, timeline, and grading: see Project 1: Deliverables, Timeline, and Grading

1. Overview

Learning goals. By the end, you should be able to:

  • Fine-tune (LoRA or full) an image captioner, a text-to-music model, a music captioner, and a text-to-image model on style-specific datasets you curated from public sources.
  • Design clean interfaces between models of different modalities and debug where information is lost.
  • Evaluate generative systems with both automatic metrics and a small human study, and know where each one fails.
  • Run controlled experiments (baselines, ablations, permutations) and draw conclusions supported by your own data.
  • Explain your modeling choices in your own words, live and on video.

2. Teams and the Pipeline

Each member owns a full four-stage loop in their own style; the team game swaps stages between members at every hop.

Teams. Groups of 3–4, assigned by course staff (mix of experience levels). Teams of 3 do the same per-person work; only the cross-member experiment is smaller.

The loop

For member m, one cycle is:

StageNameInput → outputWhat you build
CCaptionerimage → textA captioner that describes images in your style
MComposertext → musicA text-to-music model that composes in your style
LListenermusic → textA music captioner that describes clips (mood, instruments, tempo, imagery) in your style's vocabulary
GGeneratortext → imageA text-to-image model that draws in your style

Why music makes this hard

Music can't carry "a red bicycle" literally. Whatever survives the music hop has to be encoded as mood, tempo, instrumentation or texture, and decoded back by L. How much of the image makes it through, and in what form, is the central question of the project.

The telephone game

A cross-member chain uses a different member at each hop, for example CA → MB → LC → GD, then the output image feeds CB → MC → … The team decides the rotation schedule in the proposal; Experiment 3 requires at least three distinct orderings.

Interface contract (due Day 2)

Before anyone trains, the team agrees on and commits a shared interface so any member's stage can plug into any other's:

  • Images: 512 × 512 RGB PNG. Text: UTF-8, at most 60 words. Music: WAV at one agreed sample rate, 10–30 s per clip.
  • Every stage exposes the same call signature (for example stage.run(input, seed) -> output) and writes a JSON record per call: member, stage, model + checkpoint hash, seed, wall-clock time, input/output paths.
  • One command reproduces any chain from a seed image and an ordering string such as A.C,B.M,C.L,D.G.

3. Your Personal Style and Your Data

Each member picks one coherent style persona that shows up in all four stages, and curates training data for it from public datasets.

Choosing a style

Your style should be recognizable in every modality. Examples: 1940s radio noir (hard-boiled captions, smoky muted-trumpet jazz, grainy black-and-white images); 8-bit arcade (punchy all-caps captions, chiptune, pixel art); grandma's recipe card (warm rambling captions, music-box and folk-guitar lullabies, faded watercolor). Rules:

  • It must be a style the base models cannot reliably produce from a prompt alone. You will prove this with a prompted baseline (Experiment 1).
  • No living artist's or band's signature style as a target, no copyrighted songs or characters, and no real person's voice in your music except your own (or a consenting volunteer's; see Ethics).
  • Submit a style card with your proposal: one paragraph describing the style, plus examples drawn from your chosen datasets (3 captions, 3 music clips with descriptions, 3 reference images) and the datasets you plan to filter.

Curation is the work. Your style is defined by your filtering rules: tags, keywords, a CLIP/CLAP similarity threshold to a few reference examples, or a small classifier you train. Log every rule and how many items it kept, and save a random sample of 20 kept and 20 rejected items per stage. Check each dataset's license and terms; some (MusicCaps, Conceptual Captions) only link to web content you may not be able to redistribute.

Datasheet. For each dataset, submit a one-page datasheet: sources and licenses, your filtering rules and how many items each removed, how you cleaned it, time spent, known biases or gaps, and what you would add with another week.

4. Technical Requirements

Every member fine-tunes all four stages from open-weight base models, on course compute, with full training logs.

Suggested starting points

Hard requirements

  • All four stages are fine-tuned by you on your curated data. A prompt-only or zero-shot stage does not count.
  • At least one stage per member uses a technique beyond default LoRA settings that you chose for a reason you can defend (for example prior-preservation loss, caption dropout, a real/synthetic data-mixing schedule for L, a style token, negative-prompt tuning, audio augmentation designed for your music (pitch, tempo, stem mixing), melody or chroma conditioning for M).
  • No closed/paid APIs inside the pipeline. They may appear only as an extra comparison baseline.
  • Training logs for every run, including failed ones: config, loss curves (W&B or TensorBoard export), GPU type, wall-clock time, and the SHA-256 of every checkpoint used in results.
  • Reproducibility: fixed seeds, one command to rerun any chain, and a results table that regenerates from logged outputs.

Compute. Budget about [20] GPU-hours per person on [course cluster / Colab]. With LoRA, all four stages fit on one 16–24 GB GPU. Plan your runs; an out-of-budget request needs a written justification to staff by Day 7.

5. Required Experiments

Five experiments are required; every claim in your report must point to a result from one of them.

The team first picks a shared seed set of 20 images spanning at least 5 categories (people, animals, text-in-image, abstract, cluttered scenes, etc.) and commits it on Day 2.

ExperimentWhoWhat to do
E1: Does fine-tuning work?Each member, each stageCompare three variants on your held-out set: base model zero-shot, base model with your best style prompt, your fine-tuned model. Report fidelity and style metrics for all three. If fine-tuning loses to prompting on some stage, say so and explain why.
E2: Self-loop driftEach memberRun your own full loop for 5 cycles on all 20 seeds. Plot fidelity to the original image per cycle. Describe the attractor: what does your loop converge to, and why?
E3: The telephone gameTeamRun cross-member chains for at least 3 orderings (teams of 3: all orderings allowed by your rotation), 20 seeds, 3 full rotations each. Answer: Which member's style dominates after many hops? Does order matter? Which stage loses the most information, and what (if anything) of the image survives the trip through music?
E4: Who broke the message?TeamPick your most interesting E3 chain. Swap one stage at a time back to its base model (leave-one-out) and measure how drift changes. Turn the result into a per-stage attribution of information loss.
E5: Hidden seedsTeamStaff release 10 unseen images and 5 unseen seed texts on Day 12. Rerun E2 and your main E3 ordering on them within 48 hours and report whether your conclusions hold.

Per-member ablation. Each member also runs one ablation of their own choosing (for example half the training data, LoRA rank 4 vs 32, removing your special technique) on one stage, with a one-paragraph prediction written in your lab notebook before you run it.

Failure gallery. Each member contributes 5 instructive failures from their own runs. For each: the input/output, your hypothesis for why it failed, and one small test of that hypothesis.

6. Evaluation

Measure two things at every hop, fidelity (did the meaning survive?) and style (does it sound and look like you?), with metrics and with people.

Concept and mood survival

For each of the 20 seed images, the team hand-labels 3–5 literal concepts ("red bicycle", "two people", "rain") and 2–3 mood concepts ("lonely", "playful", "tense"). At each hop, record which are still present (by hand, or with a detector or classifier you validate on a sample). Expect literal concepts to die at the music hop; whether moods survive is one of the most interesting results you can report.

Human study

Course staff pair each team with a rater team; you rate theirs, they rate yours. Build the rating form yourselves (blinded, randomized order). Required tasks:

  1. Style attribution: show an image or caption, or play a clip, and ask whose style it is (chance = 1/N).
  2. Fidelity: 1–5 rating of how much of the original seed survives, shown side by side.
  3. Blind preference: fine-tuned vs prompted baseline, same input.

Collect at least 300 judgments from at least 3 raters. Report inter-rater agreement (Cohen's or Fleiss' kappa, or Krippendorff's alpha) and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for every headline number.

Metric vs human. Your report must show at least one case where an automatic metric and the human ratings disagree, and argue which one to trust there.
