Recipes vs. Records: Symbolic MIDI vs. Raw Audio

In Lesson 1, we treated music as a physical recording: a continuous stream of pressure samples capturing air vibrations, room reverberation, microphone noise, and harmonic decay.

That is a record of sound.

There is an entirely different way to represent music in machine learning: as a recipe. Instead of recording how the air moved, you record the instructions required to make the sound in the first place: which key was pressed, when it was struck, how hard it was hit, and when it was released.

This recipe format is known as MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface).

The divide between symbolic recipes and continuous records shapes modern generative audio:

  • Models that compose musical structures, counterpoint, and melodies (like MuseNet, Music Transformer, or the Bach Google Doodle) operate on symbolic recipes.
  • Models that generate realistic audio with timbre, vocals, and production polish (like MusicGen, Suno, or Stable Audio) operate on compressed records.

To design generative pipelines, you need to understand both representations, how to convert between musical intervals and physical frequencies, and how 2D musical scores flatten into 1D token sequences for Transformers.

1. Musical Notes, Octaves, and the Equal-Tempered Scale

Western music organizes continuous pitch space into discrete note names:

\[ \text{C, C}\sharp\text{, D, D}\sharp\text{, E, F, F}\sharp\text{, G, G}\sharp\text{, A, A}\sharp\text{, B} \]

Moving from one note to the immediate next note (whether white or black on a keyboard) is an interval of one semitone (or half step).

A cycle of 12 semitones forms an octave.

Perceptually, an octave jump sounds like the "same" note played higher. Physically, moving up an octave exactly doubles the frequency:

  • A3: \(220\text{ Hz}\)
  • A4 (Concert Pitch): \(440\text{ Hz}\)
  • A5: \(880\text{ Hz}\)

A standard piano keyboard contains 88 keys, spanning from the lowest note A0 up to the highest note C8.

2. The MIDI Note Formula: Mapping Integers to Hertz

Computers represent musical pitches as integer values ranging from \(0\) to \(127\).

By international MIDI standard:

  • Middle C (C4) = MIDI note \(60\)
  • Concert A (A4) = MIDI note \(69\) (\(440\text{ Hz}\))
  • Each step of \(+1\) adds one semitone.
  • Each step of \(+12\) adds one octave (\(2\times\) frequency multiplier).

Because Western instruments rely on the 12-tone equal-temperament system, every octave is divided into 12 mathematically equal frequency ratios:

\[ r = 2^{1/12} \approx 1.05946 \]

Multiplying a frequency by \(\approx 1.05946\) shifts the pitch up by exactly one semitone. Compounding this ratio 12 times yields \(r^{12} = (2^{1/12})^{12} = 2\), doubling the frequency.

The Conversion Formula

To calculate the fundamental frequency \(f\) in Hertz for any integer MIDI note \(n\), anchor the calculation at Concert A (\(n = 69\), \(f = 440\text{ Hz}\)):

\[ f(n) = 440 \times 2^{\frac{n - 69}{12}} \]

Worked Examples

Concert A (A4, \(n = 69\)):

\[ f(69) = 440 \times 2^{\frac{69 - 69}{12}} = 440 \times 2^0 = \mathbf{440.00\text{ Hz}} \]

Middle C (C4, \(n = 60\)):

\[ f(60) = 440 \times 2^{\frac{60 - 69}{12}} = 440 \times 2^{-9/12} = 440 \times 2^{-0.75} \approx \mathbf{261.63\text{ Hz}} \]

High C (C5, \(n = 72\)):

\[ f(72) = 440 \times 2^{\frac{72 - 69}{12}} = 440 \times 2^{3/12} = 440 \times 2^{0.25} \approx \mathbf{523.25\text{ Hz}} \]

Notice that \(523.25\text{ Hz} = 2 \times 261.63\text{ Hz}\). Moving \(+12\) semitones from MIDI 60 to MIDI 72 doubles the frequency.

3. The Anatomy of a MIDI Event Stream


A MIDI file does not contain a single pressure sample or Fourier coefficient. It contains a time-ordered sequence of instructions.


The two core messages that define performance data are:


    

  • NOTE_ON (channel, pitch, velocity): Triggers a note. Velocity is an integer from \(0\) to \(127\) indicating strike force. Synthesizers map higher velocity to louder volume, sharper attacks, and brighter harmonic content.
    • 

  • NOTE_OFF (channel, pitch): Releases the note, initiating its decay envelope.
    • 



Auxiliary messages handle global performance parameters, such as Tempo Changes (BPM), Control Changes (sustain pedal, mod wheel), and Program Changes (instrument patches like Acoustic Grand vs. Electric Bass).



Comparing Data Footprints: Symbolic Events vs. Raw Audio


Consider a 4-note ascending arpeggio (C4, E4, G4, C5) lasting 2.0 seconds at a tempo of 120 BPM (where 1 beat = \(0.5\text{ seconds}\)).


The symbolic stream contains only 8 lightweight events:


t = 0.00s  NOTE_ON   channel=0  pitch=60  velocity=90
t = 0.50s  NOTE_OFF  channel=0  pitch=60
t = 0.50s  NOTE_ON   channel=0  pitch=64  velocity=90
t = 1.00s  NOTE_OFF  channel=0  pitch=64
t = 1.00s  NOTE_ON   channel=0  pitch=67  velocity=90
t = 1.50s  NOTE_OFF  channel=0  pitch=67
t = 1.50s  NOTE_ON   channel=0  pitch=72  velocity=90
t = 2.00s  NOTE_OFF  channel=0  pitch=72


    

  • Symbolic Footprint: 8 messages \(\approx\) 120 bytes.
    • 

  • Raw Audio Footprint (from Lesson 1): 2.0 seconds of uncompressed 16-bit mono PCM at \(44.1\text{ kHz}\) equals \(2 \times 44,100 \times 2\text{ bytes} \approx \mathbf{176,400\text{ bytes}}\) (\(\approx 172\text{ KB}\)).
    • 



The raw audio recording requires over 1,400 times more data to store the exact same musical sequence.



  


  

    
4. The Piano Roll: Music as a 2D Matrix


Digital Audio Workstations (DAWs) and machine learning frameworks often structure MIDI as a Piano Roll matrix:


    

  • Vertical Axis (Rows): Discrete pitch steps (\(P\)).
    • 

  • Horizontal Axis (Columns): Discretized time steps (\(T\)).
    • 



A piano roll tensor \(R\) can be modeled as a binary occupancy grid or a velocity-weighted matrix:


\[ R \in \{0, 1\}^{P \times T} \quad \text{or} \quad R \in \{0, 1, \dots, 127\}^{P \times T} \]


For a full 88-key piano discretized across 512 rhythmic steps (e.g., 32 bars of sixteenth notes), the entire score fits inside an \(88 \times 512\) matrix:


\[ \text{Matrix Elements} = 88 \times 512 = 45,056\text{ cells} \]


Because most notes are not active simultaneously, this tensor is extremely sparse (predominantly zeros). Multiple active notes in a single time column naturally represent polyphonic chords.


Connection to Computer Vision: Because a piano roll is a 2D grid of values, early generative models treated composition as an image-generation problem. Models like Google Magenta's Coconet used 2D Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with masked convolutions to fill in missing notes across counterpoint voices.

5. Tokenizing Symbolic Music for Autoregressive Transformers


While CNNs can process 2D piano-roll matrices, Transformers require a 1D sequence of discrete tokens.


To train an autoregressive language model on music, researchers translate musical score events into an explicit sequence vocabulary. One widely used tokenization standard is REMI (Revamped MIDI, Huang & Yang 2020).


Instead of relying on raw millisecond timestamps, REMI defines an event vocabulary tailored to musical meter:


    

  • Bar: Marks the start of a musical measure.
    • 

  • Position: Sub-beat index within the current bar (e.g., positions \(1\) through \(16\) for sixteenth-note quantization).
    • 

  • Pitch: The MIDI note number (\(0\) to \(127\)).
    • 

  • Duration: Length of the note in quantization steps.
    • 

  • Velocity: Performance intensity bucketed into discrete levels.
    • 




Tokenizing a Single Note


Every individual note decomposes into an ordered tuple of 4 tokens:


\[ \text{[Position, Pitch, Duration, Velocity]} \]


For our 4-note arpeggio in a single measure of 4/4 time:


[Bar_0, Pos_0, Pitch_60, Dur_4, Vel_90, Pos_4, Pitch_64, Dur_4, Vel_90, Pos_8, Pitch_67, Dur_4, Vel_90, Pos_12, Pitch_72, Dur_4, Vel_90]



The Compression Advantage for Transformers


    

  • A 4-note musical phrase consumes 17 discrete tokens in REMI.
    • 

  • The total vocabulary size (bars + positions + pitches + durations + velocities) is typically a few thousand tokens, comparable to a standard Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) subword vocabulary in NLP.
    • 

  • Generating 30 seconds of symbolic music requires roughly 500 to 1,500 tokens, fitting easily within standard Transformer context windows.
    • 



However, this compression comes with an expressive tradeoff: REMI cannot capture the resonant timber of a hollow-body guitar, the breath of a vocalist, room acoustics, or subtle audio distortions. Those details exist only in the continuous acoustic record.

7. Deep Dive Case Study: Google Magenta's Coconet (The Bach Doodle)


The Google Bach Doodle demonstrated conditional symbolic generation at scale. The engine behind the Doodle is Coconet, an architecture trained on 389 four-part Bach chorales.





Try it: Google Doodle: Celebrating J.S. Bach · Background: Magenta Coconet





Mathematical Formulation


A Bach chorale consists of four separate polyphonic voices: Soprano, Alto, Tenor, and Bass. The score is represented as a 3D binary tensor of shape \((B, T, P, V)\), where:


    

  • \(B\) is batch size
    • 

  • \(T\) is the number of time frames
    • 

  • \(P\) is the pitch range (e.g., 53 semitones)
    • 

  • \(V = 4\) is the voice channels
    • 



Instead of generating autoregressively left-to-right, Coconet is trained via masked convolutional modeling (analogous to masked language models like BERT):


    

  1. Conditioning (\(c\)): The user writes a simple soprano melody on the grid.
    2. 

  2. Masking: The remaining three voices (Alto, Tenor, Bass) are masked out (set to unknown).
    3. 

  3. Infilling Iteration: The network evaluates the global context, predicts probability distributions across valid pitches for the missing cells, and fills them in a few notes at a time: it guesses, erases some of its least confident guesses, and guesses again until all four voices are complete.
    4. 



Coconet demonstrates why symbolic models excel at compositional logic: the network learns harmonic counterpoint rules directly without expending parameter capacity reconstructing waveforms or audio fidelity.

  


  

    
8. Conditional vs. Unconditional Generation


The Bach Doodle is an example of conditional generation: you supply something (a soprano melody) and the model writes music that fits it. You met the two kinds of generative model in Generative vs. Discriminative Models:


    

  • Unconditional, \(P(x)\): the model invents music from nothing but what it learned in training. A Coconet with every voice masked would write a brand-new chorale, but you would have no say in what it sounds like.
    • 

  • Conditional, \(P(x \mid c)\): the model is steered by an input \(c\). In the Doodle, \(c\) is your melody. In text-to-music systems such as MusicGen, \(c\) is a text prompt. In audio continuation, \(c\) is the first few seconds of a recording.
    • 




How does \(c\) get into the model? The same ways you saw in Multimodal Fusion Strategies: put the condition in front of the sequence as extra tokens, or let the generator look it up with cross-attention. Lesson 3 shows how a text-to-music model uses this once audio itself has been turned into tokens.
