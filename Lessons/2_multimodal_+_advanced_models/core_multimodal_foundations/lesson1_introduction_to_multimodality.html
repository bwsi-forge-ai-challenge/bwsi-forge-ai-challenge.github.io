Introduction to Multimodality & The Alignment Problem

Up to this point, our models have lived strictly inside a world of text: strings turn into tokens, tokens run through layers of attention, and new tokens spill out.

That recipe has taken modern natural language processing (NLP), the field of getting computers to read, understand, and generate human language, extraordinarily far. Text models can summarize research, draft essays, and debug code. But humans do not experience reality through a terminal feed. We make sense of the world by cross-referencing what we see, hear, and feel. A model that only reads is constantly trying to understand the physical world through a keyhole.

In this lesson, we will step outside that text box to explore multimodal AI: models that can see, listen, or synthesize across different kinds of data. Along the way, we'll break down:

  • Why text models eventually hit an informational wall.
  • What technically makes two pieces of data different "modalities."
  • The five core challenges every multimodal engineer has to solve.
  • The hardest challenge of the bunch: alignment (figuring out which slice of sound or image matches which word).

1. Why Text Eventually Hits a Ceiling

Internet-scale text collections contain vast amounts of human thought, but relying exclusively on text leaves three distinct blind spots:

Reporting bias: we don't write down the obvious

Nobody tweets, "My coffee mug has a solid bottom," or "When I drop my keys, they hit the floor." We only spend effort writing down things that are unusual, interesting, or contested. Because language models learn purely from what people choose to document, they inherit this skew. They can explain quantum entanglement while occasionally hallucinating basic facts about physical spaces or gravity.

The bottleneck of turning reality into words

Text is an incredible summary tool, but it is still just a summary. Think about reading the lyrics to a song versus listening to the actual track: the words alone tell you nothing about the tempo, the vocal rasp, or the bassline. When we force real-world phenomena through a text-only pipeline, we throw away most of the original resolution. Audio tone, pixel-level textures, and raw sensor readings simply don't fit into a sentence without losing what made them informative in the first place.

The "grounding" problem

In an earlier lesson, we saw that a text model represents a word as a vector embedded near other words used in similar contexts. To a purely textual model, cherry is just a high-dimensional vector positioned near pie, red, and sweet. It has never actually "seen" red. Grounding means anchoring abstract symbols (words) to real-world sensory phenomena (the light spectrum striking an image sensor). Training on images alongside text lets an architecture bridge that gap.

2. What Counts as a "Modality"?

We tend to think of modalities intuitively as our human senses: sight, sound, text, touch. In machine learning, a modality is defined by how the signal is captured, organized, and stored.

Even if two inputs tell the exact same story, they belong to different modalities if their underlying computational structures differ:

  • An essay saved as a .txt file is a 1D sequence of discrete integer token IDs drawn from a fixed dictionary.
  • A screenshot of that exact same essay is a 3D numeric array (Height × Width × 3 Color Channels), where every pixel is a continuous value between 0 and 255. A language model cannot read it until a vision encoder processes those spatial pixel patterns.
  • A spectrogram of someone reading the essay translates pressure waves over time into a frequency-intensity map. Though it began as audio, its model-facing form is an image grid.

When building an autonomous vehicle, cameras deliver pixel arrays, lidar returns variable-density 3D coordinates, and radar streams frequency shifts. The core puzzle of multimodal AI is designing architectures capable of fusing these fundamentally incompatible representations.

3. How Brains Do It: Conflict, Bias, and Compensation

To understand how models wrestle with multiple streams, look at how the human brain solves the same problem. When senses feed into your cortex, your brain doesn't just average them:

  • Conflict resolution (the McGurk effect): If you watch a video of a speaker mouthing "ga" while the audio track plays "ba", your auditory system does not flip a coin. It fuses the conflicting cues and forces your conscious perception into hearing "da". When sensory inputs contradict each other, multimodal systems need explicit rules or learned strategies for which modality to trust.
  • Spatial dominance (the ventriloquist effect): When a ventriloquist speaks without parting their lips while wiggling a puppet, you perceive the voice as originating from the puppet's mouth. Vision has much higher spatial precision than hearing, so your brain lets visual cues dictate spatial source.
  • Redundancy and gap filling: In a noisy restaurant, reading someone's lips dramatically lowers the effort required to parse their speech. When one modality's signal-to-noise ratio drops, another modality steps in to fill the gaps.

4. The Five Core Multimodal Challenges

Researchers break down multimodal machine learning into five central technical challenges:

  • Representation: How do you map wildly different inputs (like a 2D image and a string of text) into vector embeddings that can be directly compared? (e.g., training encoders so a photo of a golden retriever and the text string "a golden retriever" end up close to each other in vector space).
  • Translation: How do you map an input in modality A directly into a brand-new sample in modality B? (e.g., text-to-image diffusion, speech-to-text, or image captioning).
  • Alignment: How do you pinpoint corresponding sub-components across two modalities? (e.g., matching the phrase "the silver bicycle" to the specific bounding box or pixels containing that bike).
  • Fusion: How do you merge information from two distinct modalities into a single downstream decision? (e.g., combining a patient's vital telemetry with clinical notes to predict readmission).
  • Co-learning: How do you leverage an abundance of data in one modality to help a model make sense of a scarce, niche modality? (e.g., transferring knowledge from billions of internet photos to help interpret rare 3D medical CT scans).

5. The Alignment Problem Up Close

Before an architecture can fuse modalities together, it must resolve alignment: establishing which piece of signal A corresponds to which piece of signal B.

Alignment is deceptive because it rarely comes with clean 1:1 boundaries:

The clocks don't match

Consider a cooking video where a chef says, "Fold in the whipped cream."

  • Video: Recorded at 30 frames/sec (each frame spans ~33 ms).
  • Audio: Recorded at 16,000 samples/sec.
  • Transcript: The word "fold" is a single token, taking roughly 300 ms to say.

That single token corresponds simultaneously to ~10 visual frames and ~4,800 audio samples. The model has to reconcile these disparate temporal grains without human hints.

Nobody labels the sub-components

Web-scraped datasets pair whole images with whole captions or alt-text. Nobody draws bounding boxes around every noun in a billion captions. The network must infer on its own that the visual pattern of a handle and spout corresponds to the phrase "ceramic teapot."

Information is often one-sided

Streams rarely line up with matching facts point-for-point. An image might show a park bench, the trees behind it, and the color of the grass, while the caption only mentions "a golden retriever catching a frisbee." Conversely, a caption might say "Charlie playing in his favorite park," giving background context that is impossible to derive from the pixels alone. A model cannot expect every entity in one modality to have a counterpart in the other. It has to figure out what to align, what to ignore, and what context is unique to a single channel.

How models solve it: explicit vs. implicit alignment

  • Explicit alignment: The model is directly trained to output alignment mappings.
    • Forced alignment: Predicting the start and end timestamps of every spoken phoneme given an audio stream and transcript.
    • Phrase grounding: Predicting the exact pixel mask or bounding box associated with an input phrase.
  • Implicit alignment: The model learns alignment organically as an intermediate byproduct of solving a larger objective.
    • Cross-attention: In modern vision-language models, an image is split into a grid of patches (each treated much like a token vector). When generating text via cross-attention, the model's text queries attend directly across the image's patch keys. When predicting the token "dog", the attention weights over the dog's pixel patches spike naturally, not because anyone explicitly told it where the dog was, but because attending to those patches minimized next-token prediction loss.

Hands-On Activity: The Multimodal Audit & Cross-Modal Drift

This quick exercise illustrates why alignment and cross-modal translation are non-trivial.

The human modality audit

Pick a short 10-second clip from a movie, documentary, or daily life featuring speech, ambient noise, and movement. Create a table mapping the information:

  1. Text stream: Transcribe the spoken dialogue verbatim.
  2. Audio-only stream: Close your eyes. Note everything you hear that the text omitted (tone, volume, footstep textures, background hums, emotional tension).
  3. Vision-only stream: Mute the clip. List every piece of spatial context that has zero sound or mention in dialogue (facial expressions, lighting, room size, colors).
  4. Identify the alignment anchors: Find one moment where all three streams anchor onto the same event (e.g., an actor bangs a desk while yelling a word). Note how many frames and samples that single beat spans.

Core Takeaways

  • Words aren't enough: Text-only models miss unspoken common-sense physics, lose unwritten acoustic and visual detail, and struggle with semantic grounding.
  • Modalities are computational formats: Data types are defined by their structure: discrete token lists, continuous 2D/3D pixel arrays, 1D sample streams, or irregular 3D point clouds.
  • The 5 foundational pillars: Multimodal research targets Representation, Translation, Alignment, Fusion, or Co-learning.
  • Alignment is foundational: Modalities run on different sampling rates, internet data lacks fine-grained part labels, and channels rarely overlap cleanly. Modern systems resolve this using mechanisms like cross-attention to uncover implicit correspondences across modalities.
