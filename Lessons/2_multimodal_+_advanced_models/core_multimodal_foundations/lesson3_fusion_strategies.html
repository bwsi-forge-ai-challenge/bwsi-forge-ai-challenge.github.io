Multimodal Fusion Strategies & Architecture Paradigms

In the previous lesson, we looked at how to map different inputs into a shared space. Once an image has been cut into patch vectors and a sound clip has been turned into a sequence of feature vectors, an important design question comes up: at what point in the network should these separate streams merge?

Merge them at the very first layer, and a single broken or missing sensor can derail everything the network computes after it. Wait until the final prediction, and the model can no longer discover meaning that only shows up in the interplay between senses.

This lesson walks through the three main ways multimodal systems combine their inputs, early, late, and intermediate fusion, along with how each one fails and what goes wrong when you train them.

What you should already know. This lesson builds on:

  • Joint representations and concatenation: Multimodal Representation & Latent Spaces
  • Queries, keys, values, and softmax in attention: Attention: The Key Innovation
  • The sigmoid function: Classification and Logistic Regression

1. The Fusion Taxonomy

Think of a multimodal network as a set of parallel pipelines, one for each input. Data moves forward through each pipeline layer by layer, which is called the forward pass. The fusion point is the stage where information is first allowed to pass from one pipeline to another.

  • Early fusion (feature level): the features are combined at the very first layer. A single shared backbone, meaning the main body of the network, processes the combined input from the start.
  • Late fusion (decision level): a separate network for each modality runs all the way to its own prediction. Those predictions are then merged by averaging, voting, or a small classifier.
  • Intermediate (hybrid) fusion: each modality first passes through its own layers to build mid-level features. After that, cross-modal layers such as attention blocks or learned gates let the modalities exchange information.

2. Early Fusion: Merging Features

Early fusion combines the raw inputs, or simple features of them, right at the entrance of the network. The most common way is to concatenate the visual features \(\mathbf{x}_v\) and the audio features \(\mathbf{x}_a\) into one vector, just like the concatenation in Lesson 2:

\[ \mathbf{x}_{\text{fused}} = \left[\, \mathbf{x}_v \,\Vert\, \mathbf{x}_a \,\right] \]

Example: audio-visual speech recognition

A classic use case is audio-visual speech recognition: transcribing speech using both the sound and a video of the speaker's lips. The audio is usually represented as a spectrogram, the picture of sound you met in Lesson 1 (the audio unit covers it in detail). Every 20 milliseconds, the system takes one slice of the spectrogram and concatenates it with a vector describing the shape of the speaker's lips at that same moment.

Because the two signals are joined from the first layer, even the network's earliest filters can pick up tightly synchronized events. For example, a speaker's lips press together a fraction of a second before the burst of sound in a "p" or "b." A network that only combined the two streams near the end would blur away those millisecond-level offsets.

Trade-offs and failure modes

  • Strength: fine-grained interactions. It can learn links in time and space that get washed out once each modality has been summarized by many layers, and only one network has to be trained and stored.
  • Strict synchronization. Every stream has to be lined up on the same clock, or resampled onto a shared time grid, before the vectors can be stacked.
  • A missing input breaks everything. The first layer expects complete vectors from every modality. If a camera feed drops out or a microphone is unplugged, that part of the input becomes zeros or random noise. This is out-of-distribution data: a kind of input the network never saw during training. Because it enters at layer zero, it flows through every weight matrix after it, and the result is often a confident, completely wrong answer instead of an honest "I'm not sure."

3. Late Fusion: Combining Independent Decisions

Why do we need to merge anything at all if individual models can already make predictions on their own?

Say you're building a security monitor for a quiet library. You set up a high-resolution camera and a directional microphone. You train a computer vision model to look at video frames and output the probability of an intruder, and you train an audio model to listen to sound clips and do the same.

At 2:00 AM, the vision model spots a flashlight beam moving across the room and reports an 85% chance of an intruder. At the same second, the microphone hears complete silence and reports a 10% chance.

The system can't sound one alarm and stay quiet at the same time. Whatever happens next (calling security, logging the event, doing nothing) needs a single answer: sound the alarm, or stay quiet.

That is the motivation behind late fusion, also called decision-level fusion. Instead of forcing raw pixels and raw sound waves to interact inside one big network, each modality runs through its own specialized model. Once every model has reached its own verdict, we merge those verdicts into one final score:

\[ \hat{y} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} w_m \cdot \hat{y}_m \]

Here's what each term means:

  • \(M\) is the number of modalities you're combining. In the library, \(M = 2\): vision and audio.
  • \(\hat{y}_m\) is the standalone verdict from modality \(m\). Here \(\hat{y}_{\text{vision}} = 0.85\) and \(\hat{y}_{\text{audio}} = 0.10\).
  • \(w_m\) is how much we trust that sensor's opinion. The weights are required to add up to 1 (\(\sum w_m = 1\)), so the final answer is still a valid probability.
  • \(\hat{y}\) is the merged score the system actually acts on.

Deciding how much to trust each sensor

The main design question in late fusion is how to set the weights \(w_m\). There are three common approaches:

  • Equal trust (uniform averaging). If both sensors are about equally dependable, give them the same say, \(w_{\text{vision}} = w_{\text{audio}} = 0.5\). The final score is a plain average: \[ \hat{y} = (0.5 \times 0.85) + (0.5 \times 0.10) = 0.475 \]
  • Learned trust (validation tuning). Suppose past data shows the camera catches 95% of incidents, while the microphone is often fooled by wind or the air conditioning. You can tune the weights on held-out validation data and end up with something like \(w_{\text{vision}} = 0.8\) and \(w_{\text{audio}} = 0.2\): \[ \hat{y} = (0.8 \times 0.85) + (0.2 \times 0.10) = 0.70 \]
  • Dynamic trust (confidence gating). If a sensor can tell that its own input is bad (say the camera sees total darkness), the system can set \(w_{\text{vision}} = 0\) on the fly and let audio make the call alone.

The real advantage: fault isolation

The biggest practical benefit of late fusion is that the modalities never touch while features are being extracted.

If the microphone wire gets cut, the audio model has no signal, but the vision model keeps processing video as if nothing happened. You can unplug, retrain, or replace the vision network with a brand-new architecture next month without changing a single line of code or retraining a single weight in the audio pipeline. The two models can even run on different chips: a phone might run the audio model on a small low-power processor and the vision model on a chip designed for neural networks.

The blind spot: missing the context

Late fusion is modular and resilient, but it has one fundamental limitation: it can't see any interaction between the modalities.

Think about how people communicate. Someone says the word "brilliant" while rolling their eyes:

  • The text model reads "brilliant" and predicts a 95% chance the sentiment is positive.
  • The vision model sees an eye-roll and predicts a 10% chance it's positive.
  • A late-fusion average blends these into \((0.95 + 0.10)/2 \approx 0.53\), and concludes the speaker is roughly neutral.

The system misses the sarcasm completely, because sarcasm doesn't live in the text alone or in the eye-roll alone. The meaning is in the contradiction between the two. Late fusion keeps the models separate until after they've each made up their minds, so neither one ever gets the chance to notice that the other sensor disagrees with it.

4. Intermediate and Hybrid Fusion

Intermediate fusion avoids both extremes. Each modality is first processed by its own encoder into mid-level features, and then the modalities interact through dedicated cross-modal layers. Most modern multimodal models work this way. Two mechanisms are especially common.

Mechanism A: cross-attention

Recall the Attention lesson. In self-attention, the queries (\(Q\), what each token is looking for), keys (\(K\), what each token offers), and values (\(V\), the information it passes along) all come from the same sequence. In cross-attention, the queries come from one modality while the keys and values come from another:

\[ \text{Attention}(Q_{\text{text}}, K_{\text{image}}, V_{\text{image}}) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_{\text{text}}\, K_{\text{image}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V_{\text{image}} \]

This is the same attention formula you already know; only the sources of \(Q\), \(K\), and \(V\) have changed. (\(d_k\) is the length of each key vector, and dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the scores from getting too large.) In a model writing a caption for an image, each text token's query is scored against the keys of every image patch. When the model is about to write the name of an object, the attention weights concentrate on the patches that contain that object, like the dog example in Lesson 1, and those patches' features flow directly into the text being generated.

The cost of cross-attention

Every query is compared with every key. For \(N\) text queries and \(M\) image-patch keys, each attention head computes \(N \times M\) dot products. Computer scientists write this with "big-O" notation, which describes how the cost grows as the inputs get bigger:

\[ \mathcal{O}(N \cdot M) \]

Worked example. 64 text tokens attend to an image split into a \(14 \times 14\) grid of patches:

\[ M = 14 \times 14 = 196 \text{ patches}, \qquad 64 \times 196 = \mathbf{12{,}544} \text{ dot products per head} \]

Now double the image resolution, so the grid doubles along each side to \(28 \times 28 = 784\) patches:

\[ 64 \times 784 = \mathbf{50{,}176} \text{ dot products per head} \]

Doubling the width and height quadruples the cost, because the number of patches grows with the square of the resolution.

Because that growth can quickly use up memory, high-resolution models often shrink the number of image tokens before cross-attention, for example by averaging neighboring patches together (pooling) or passing them through a small projection layer.

Mechanism B: gated multimodal units

Fixed mixing weights work badly when input quality changes from one moment to the next. A camera lens can be blocked by snow while the radar keeps working, or an image can be blurry while its caption is perfectly clear. A rule like "always trust vision 50%" can't adapt. A gated multimodal unit (GMU; Arevalo et al., 2017) instead computes a fresh set of mixing weights for every example, based on what the inputs look like:

It works in three steps:

  1. Candidate features. The visual input \(\mathbf{x}_v\) and text input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) each pass through their own layer. \(\tanh\) is an activation function that squashes every number into the range \(-1\) to \(1\): \[ \mathbf{h}_v = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_v), \qquad \mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_t \mathbf{x}_t) \]
  2. The gate. The two inputs are concatenated and passed through a layer with a sigmoid \(\sigma\), which squashes every number into the range 0 to 1. The result, \(\mathbf{z}\), has one value between 0 and 1 for each feature: \[ \mathbf{z} = \sigma\big(\mathbf{W}_z [\,\mathbf{x}_v \,\Vert\, \mathbf{x}_t\,]\big) \]
  3. The blend. The output takes a fraction \(z\) of each visual feature and the remaining \(1 - z\) of the matching text feature. The symbol \(\odot\) means multiply entry by entry: \[ \mathbf{h}_{\text{fused}} = \mathbf{z} \odot \mathbf{h}_v + (\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{z}) \odot \mathbf{h}_t \]

If an image is dark or blurry, the gate learns to output values near 0, turning down the noisy visual features and leaning on the text. If the text is garbled while the image is sharp, the gate moves toward 1 and leans on vision. Nobody writes these rules by hand: the gate's weights \(\mathbf{W}_z\) are learned by backpropagation along with everything else, because trusting the clearer input lowers the loss.

5. Training Problems: Modality Collapse

Training an early or intermediate fusion network end to end, meaning all the parts at once with one loss, often runs into a problem called modality collapse or modality laziness (Wang et al., 2020). You met a version of it in Lesson 2 with concatenation.

Why it happens: modalities learn at different speeds

In video action classification (labeling what is happening in a clip), the audio often contains a quick, obvious clue, like the crash of bowling pins. The video frames, on the other hand, are full of detail that takes many more training steps to make sense of.

Gradient descent takes the path of least resistance. The weights on the fast-learning audio side improve first, and once the audio clues explain most of the training labels, the overall loss is already low. That leaves very little gradient flowing into the slower vision side. Writing \(\mathbf{g}\) for the gradient reaching each branch and \(\Vert \cdot \Vert\) for its size:

\[ \Vert \mathbf{g}_{\text{vision}} \Vert \ll \Vert \mathbf{g}_{\text{audio}} \Vert \]

The vision encoder barely gets any training signal and stalls. You end up with a "multimodal" model that is really an audio-only model carrying millions of unused vision weights.

How to prevent it

  • Modality dropout. During training, randomly replace an entire modality's input with zeros (or a special learned "missing" vector) with some probability \(p\), usually between 0.1 and 0.3. The network can't rely on any single input, so every encoder has to learn something useful. As a bonus, the model is already used to a missing sensor when it is deployed.
  • Auxiliary unimodal losses. Attach a small extra prediction head to each encoder, give each head its own loss, and add them to the main loss: \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{fused}} + \alpha\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{vision}} + \beta\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{audio}} \] Here \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are small weights you choose. Because each encoder also has to make a prediction on its own, a strong training signal reaches every encoder no matter what the fusion layers end up relying on.
  • Gradient blending. Wang et al.'s own fix: track how fast each modality is learning, and scale each branch's gradients so the slower modality keeps getting a useful signal.

6. Architecture Decision Matrix

Use this table when deciding where to put the fusion point in a system:

Early fusionIntermediate fusionLate fusion
Cross-modal interactionsHigh: catches precise timing between synchronized signalsVery high: each token can attend to exactly the parts of the other modality it needsLow: only averages independent predictions

UpgradesHard: changing one sensor means retraining everythingModerate: encoders can be pretrained separately and connected with projection layers, like the projection heads in Lesson 2Easy: swap out any single model

Exercise 1: the cost of cross-attention

Question: A drone navigates using a forward camera image of \(224 \times 224\) pixels, split into \(16 \times 16\)-pixel patches, and a microphone array that produces 64 audio tokens. A fusion block runs cross-attention with the audio tokens as queries and the image patches as keys.

  1. How many patches does the image produce?
  2. How many query–key dot products does one attention head compute?
  3. What is the shape of the attention-weight matrix \(\mathbf{A}\)?

Solution:

\[ \frac{224}{16} \times \frac{224}{16} = 14 \times 14 = \mathbf{196} \text{ patches} \]

Each of the 64 audio queries is compared with each of the 196 image keys:

\[ 64 \times 196 = \mathbf{12{,}544} \text{ dot products} \]

The attention matrix has one row per query and one column per key:

\[ \mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 196} \]
Exercise 2: a sensor failure

Question: A wildfire detection station estimates risk using an infrared thermal camera and a humidity sensor. Thick smoke blinds the thermal camera, so its images become random noise. The humidity sensor still works and reports severe drought: 8% relative humidity. Compare what happens under early fusion, late fusion, and a gated multimodal unit.

Solution:

  • Early fusion: the noise from the blinded camera enters the first layer alongside the humidity reading. It corrupts the hidden features from there on, and the network can no longer reliably use the humidity signal.
  • Late fusion: the thermal model is unsure (\(P \approx 0.50\)), while the humidity model reports extreme risk (\(P \approx 0.98\)). The average, \((0.50 + 0.98)/2 \approx 0.74\), is still high enough to trigger the alarm.
  • Gated multimodal unit: the gate recognizes that the thermal features are noise and turns them down (\(\mathbf{z} \to 0\)), so the clear humidity features drive the prediction.

Key Takeaways

  • A spectrum of choices: where you fuse trades detailed, low-level interactions (early fusion) against modularity and fault tolerance (late fusion).
  • Cross-attention lets one modality look things up in another: queries from one sequence attend directly to keys and values from the other, at a cost of \(\mathcal{O}(N \cdot M)\).
  • Gates decide how much to trust each input: gated multimodal units learn a per-example blend based on how reliable each signal looks.
  • Watch out for modality collapse: when one modality learns faster, the network can ignore the others. Modality dropout, auxiliary losses, and gradient blending keep every encoder learning.
