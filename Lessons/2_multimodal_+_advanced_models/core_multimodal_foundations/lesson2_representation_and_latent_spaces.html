Making Senses Talk: Multimodal Representation & Latent Spaces

In the previous lesson, we looked at how different signals enter an AI system: sensors record streams of measurements, cameras capture 2D grids of light intensity, and language models read sequences of token IDs. Before a model can reason about what it sees and reads, it has to turn all of these inputs into lists of numbers called feature vectors.

Here is the problem: vectors that come out of separate encoders can't be compared out of the box. If you pass a photo of an espresso machine into a vision model and the words "steaming milk" into a language model, you can't just take the dot product of the two outputs or measure the distance between them. They live in completely different numerical worlds.

In this lesson, we'll cover:

  • Why embeddings from different models clash.
  • The two main ways to bridge them: joint representations and coordinated spaces.
  • The math behind contrastive learning (the InfoNCE loss), which powers systems like CLIP.
  • When to choose fast search over richer reasoning.

You will implement the same mechanics in PyTorch in this unit's assignment.

What you should already know. This lesson builds directly on earlier material. If any of these feel rusty, skim them first:

  • Vectors, dot products, and vector length (norm): Vectors
  • Matrix multiplication and transposes: Matrices
  • Embeddings and cosine similarity: Embeddings: Meaning in Vector Space
  • Softmax and cross-entropy loss: Multi-Layer Perceptrons and Classification and Logistic Regression

1. The Dimensional and Semantic Wall

To turn an image or a phrase into a vector, we pass it through an encoder: a neural network trained to pull out high-level patterns. Recall from the Embeddings lesson that the result is an embedding, a list of numbers that captures the meaning of the input. Each modality needs its own encoder, because a sentence and a photo have completely different raw forms.

  • For text, the encoder is usually a transformer like the one you built up in the Foundations module. It turns tokens into contextual embeddings.
  • For images, the encoder is a separate network trained on pictures. For now, treat it as a black box that takes in a grid of pixels and returns a vector. Later units look at how image encoders work inside.

Each encoder outputs a single vector that summarizes its input. But if you try to compare the two vectors directly, you hit two roadblocks right away:

Roadblock A: the shapes don't match

The two encoders are built and trained independently, so nothing forces their outputs to be the same size. In the base CLIP model (a well-known image-and-text model from OpenAI), the image encoder outputs 768 numbers and the text encoder outputs 512. In math notation, \(\mathbf{z}_{\text{img}} \in \mathbb{R}^{768}\) and \(\mathbf{z}_{\text{text}} \in \mathbb{R}^{512}\), where \(\mathbb{R}^{768}\) ("R to the 768") just means "a list of 768 real numbers." A dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them up, so it simply isn't defined between a list of 768 numbers and a list of 512.

Roadblock B: the coordinates mean different things

Even if you forced both encoders to output 512 numbers, comparing them would still fail. Coordinate 42 in the image encoder might respond to fine vertical textures or the sharpness of an edge. Coordinate 42 in the text encoder might track whether a sentence is in the past tense. Each model learned its own meaning for each coordinate while training on its own task, so the axes share no meaning at all.

The fix is a multimodal latent space: a shared coordinate system designed so that related concepts land near each other, no matter which modality they came from. ("Latent" just means hidden: these are the model's internal vectors, not the raw input.) There are two main ways to build one.

2. Strategy A: Joint Representations

A joint architecture blends the input streams into a single vector \(\mathbf{z}_{\text{joint}}\), as early or as deeply as it can, then hands that vector to a prediction head: a small network on top, like the output layer of an MLP, that makes the final prediction, such as a class label or a number.

\[ \mathbf{z}_{\text{joint}} = f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{image}},\ \mathbf{x}_{\text{text}},\ \mathbf{x}_{\text{sensor}}) \]

Here \(f\) is whatever combining operation we choose. There are two common options.

Option 1: Early concatenation

The most straightforward approach is to normalize each feature vector and glue them end to end. The symbol \(\Vert\) means "stick these vectors together":

\[ \mathbf{z}_{\text{concat}} = \left[\, \mathbf{z}_{\text{image}} \,\Vert\, \mathbf{z}_{\text{text}} \,\Vert\, \mathbf{z}_{\text{sensor}} \,\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{image}} + d_{\text{text}} + d_{\text{sensor}}} \]

Each \(d\) is the length of one modality's vector. If the image vector has 128 entries, the text vector has 64, and a vector of sensor readings has 64, the concatenated vector has \(128 + 64 + 64 = 256\) entries.

Concatenation is simple and cheap, but it has a big flaw: it doesn't model how the modalities interact. It places the numbers side by side and leaves the later layers to work out how they relate. In practice, those layers often take the easy route and lean almost entirely on whichever modality is easiest to learn from (often text), while ignoring the others. This failure is called modality collapse.

Option 2: Tensor fusion

Sometimes the meaning only shows up when two signals are combined. Someone writing "Oh, fantastic" next to a photo of their flooded basement isn't thrilled. The words alone could be sincere, and the photo alone is just a basement, but together they are clearly sarcastic.

Tensor fusion (Zadeh et al., 2017) captures these combinations with the outer product (\(\otimes\)). A dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them into one number. An outer product instead multiplies every entry of one vector by every entry of the other and keeps all the results in a grid.

Before taking the outer product, we append a 1 to each vector. That small step means the grid keeps each modality's original values alongside the combinations:

\[ \mathbf{z}_{\text{tensor}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{z}_v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \otimes \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{z}_t \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Let's trace a small example where \(\mathbf{z}_v = [v_1, v_2]\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t = [t_1, t_2]\). Appending a 1 to each gives vectors of length 3, and their outer product is a 3 × 3 grid:

\[ \mathbf{z}_{\text{tensor}} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} t_1 & t_2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 t_1 & v_1 t_2 & v_1 \\ v_2 t_1 & v_2 t_2 & v_2 \\ t_1 & t_2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Notice what this grid contains:

  • The top-left 2 × 2 block holds every product \(v_i t_j\) of a visual feature and a text feature. These bimodal terms directly model how a visual feature changes the meaning of a text feature, and the other way around.
  • The right column keeps the original visual values \(v_1, v_2\).
  • The bottom row keeps the original text values \(t_1, t_2\).
  • The 1 in the corner is a constant bias term.

The grid is then flattened into one long vector and passed to the prediction head. With three modalities, the outer product becomes a 3-D block of numbers rather than a grid, and it also contains every three-way (trimodal) product.

The catch: the size explodes

Tensor fusion sounds ideal, but it quickly hits a wall. The flattened tensor's size is the product of all the vector lengths, each plus 1. The symbol \(\prod\) means "multiply all of these together":

\[ d_{\text{tensor}} = \prod_{m=1}^{M} (d_m + 1) \]

With image, text, and sensor vectors of just 64 features each, the flattened tensor has \(65 \times 65 \times 65 = \mathbf{274{,}625}\) entries. Connecting it to a modest hidden layer of 128 neurons would take \(274{,}625 \times 128 \approx 35\) million weights for that first layer alone, which quickly leads to memory problems and overfitting.

Because of this, modern systems rarely use raw tensor fusion. They capture interactions with low-rank approximations instead (Low-Rank Multimodal Fusion approximates the big tensor with much smaller pieces), or with cross-attention layers, which you will meet in the next lesson.

3. Strategy B: Coordinated Spaces (the CLIP Approach)

Instead of mashing images and text into one tangled vector, coordinated spaces take a cleaner approach: keep each modality separate, but teach them to share a common map. If you picture the shared space as a room, the goal is to place a photo of a golden retriever and the phrase "a playful dog" in the same corner.

The biggest payoff of keeping them separate is fast search at web scale, also called retrieval. Because the two encoders run independently, you can run 100 million catalog photos through the image encoder once, save their vectors in an index, and never touch those raw images again. When a user searches for "red running shoes," you only run the text encoder on those three words. Then you compare that one text vector against the saved image vectors using dot products. Libraries such as Facebook AI's Faiss are built to do this over millions of vectors in a few milliseconds, without checking every single one. You never have to run a heavy joint network on 100 million image–text pairs.

Turning the mismatched encoder outputs into a shared search space takes three steps.

Step 1: Projection heads (matching the shapes and lining up the axes)

Remember the starting problem: the image encoder outputs 768 numbers, while the text encoder outputs 512. Worse, coordinate 42 means something like "sharp edges" on the image side and something like "verb tense" on the text side.

To fix both problems, we attach a small trainable layer called a projection head to the end of each encoder: one for images and one for text. It is usually a single linear layer, a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) and a bias vector \(\mathbf{b}\), the same building block as one layer of an MLP. (Some models use a small two-layer MLP instead, but the idea is the same.)

\[ \text{Image head:} \quad \mathbf{p}_v = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{z}_v + \mathbf{b}_v \] \[ \text{Text head:} \quad \mathbf{p}_t = \mathbf{W}_t \mathbf{z}_t + \mathbf{b}_t \]
  • \(\mathbf{z}_v\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) are the encoder outputs: the image vector (768 numbers for CLIP) and the text vector (512 numbers).
  • \(\mathbf{W}_v\), \(\mathbf{W}_t\), \(\mathbf{b}_v\), and \(\mathbf{b}_t\) are each head's weights and biases. These are learned during training.
  • \(\mathbf{p}_v\) and \(\mathbf{p}_t\) are the outputs: the image and the text as vectors in the shared space.

How does this guarantee that both vectors come out the same size? It comes down to the shape rule for multiplying a matrix by a vector, from the Matrices lesson: the matrix's columns must match the input length, and its rows decide the output length.

  • First we choose the size of the shared space. Call it \(d_e\); for CLIP, \(d_e = 512\).
  • The image matrix \(\mathbf{W}_v\) has shape \(512 \times 768\). It takes in 768 image features and compresses them down to 512.
  • The text matrix \(\mathbf{W}_t\) has shape \(512 \times 512\). The length stays 512, but the matrix recombines the text features so that their meaning can line up with the image side.
\[ \underbrace{\mathbf{p}_v}_{512} = \underbrace{\mathbf{W}_v}_{512 \times 768}\ \underbrace{\mathbf{z}_v}_{768} + \underbrace{\mathbf{b}_v}_{512} \] \[ \underbrace{\mathbf{p}_t}_{512} = \underbrace{\mathbf{W}_t}_{512 \times 512}\ \underbrace{\mathbf{z}_t}_{512} + \underbrace{\mathbf{b}_t}_{512} \]

Now both vectors are exactly 512 numbers long, which fixes Roadblock A. Roadblock B is fixed during training: backpropagation tunes the two matrices until coordinate 1, coordinate 2, and so on mean the same thing for images and for text. The InfoNCE loss in the next section is what drives that training. The matrices are also where the heads' trainable weights live: \(512 \times 768 = 393{,}216\) for \(\mathbf{W}_v\) and \(512 \times 512 = 262{,}144\) for \(\mathbf{W}_t\).

Step 2: Normalization (direction, not length)

Every vector has two properties: a length (also called magnitude) and a direction. In deep learning, a vector's raw length often tracks unhelpful surface traits, like how bright an image is or how many adjectives a sentence has. We don't want a brightly lit photo of a cat to look unrelated to the phrase "a sleeping cat" just because its vector happens to be twice as long.

To strip away that scale, we divide each vector by its own length, its \(L_2\) norm from the Vectors lesson. This is called \(L_2\) normalization, and it forces every vector to have a length of exactly 1:

\[ \mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{p}_v}{\Vert \mathbf{p}_v \Vert_2}, \qquad \mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{p}_t}{\Vert \mathbf{p}_t \Vert_2} \]

In 2D, the tips of these unit vectors all land on a circle of radius 1. In 512 dimensions, they land on the surface of a 512-dimensional sphere of radius 1, called the unit hypersphere. Only direction is left.

Once every vector has length 1, the cosine similarity you met in the Embeddings lesson simplifies to a plain dot product, because the denominator becomes \(1 \times 1\):

\[ S_C(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\Vert \mathbf{u} \Vert_2 \, \Vert \mathbf{v} \Vert_2} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{1 \times 1} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \sum_{k=1}^{d_e} u_k v_k \]

The score always lies between \(-1\) and \(+1\):

  • \(+1\): the vectors point the same way (the same meaning).
  • \(0\): the vectors are at right angles, or orthogonal (unrelated meanings).
  • \(-1\): the vectors point in opposite directions.

Step 3: The batch similarity matrix (scoring every pair at once)

During training, we don't look at one pair at a time. We pass in a mini-batch of \(N\) images and their \(N\) captions, where photo 1 belongs with caption 1, photo 2 with caption 2, and so on. To teach the model what matches and what doesn't, we score every image against every caption in the batch, not just its own. That is \(N \times N\) scores, and instead of looping over every pair, a single matrix multiplication computes all of them at once.

A small example. Take a batch of \(N = 3\): a dog photo, a pizza photo, and a car photo, with the captions "a dog," "a pizza," and "a red car." After the projection heads and normalization, each one is a unit vector. To keep the arithmetic easy, pretend the shared space has only 3 dimensions and the model is already well trained:

Image vectorsText vectors
\(\mathbf{u}_1\) (dog photo) \(= [1,\ 0,\ 0]\)\(\mathbf{v}_1\) ("a dog") \(= [0.96,\ 0.28,\ 0]\)
\(\mathbf{u}_2\) (pizza photo) \(= [0,\ 1,\ 0]\)\(\mathbf{v}_2\) ("a pizza") \(= [0,\ 0.96,\ 0.28]\)
\(\mathbf{u}_3\) (car photo) \(= [0,\ 0,\ 1]\)\(\mathbf{v}_3\) ("a red car") \(= [0.28,\ 0,\ 0.96]\)

You can check that each text vector has length 1: \(0.96^2 + 0.28^2 = 0.9216 + 0.0784 = 1\).

Stack the vectors into matrices. Put the image vectors in the rows of a matrix \(\mathbf{I}\), and the text vectors in the rows of a matrix \(\mathbf{T}\). Each matrix has one row per example (\(N\) rows) and one column per dimension of the shared space (\(d_e\) columns), so both have shape \(N \times d_e\):

\[ \mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{u}_1 \\ \mathbf{u}_2 \\ \mathbf{u}_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_1 \\ \mathbf{v}_2 \\ \mathbf{v}_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.96 & 0.28 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.96 & 0.28 \\ 0.28 & 0 & 0.96 \end{bmatrix} \]

Multiply. Each entry of a matrix product is a row of the first matrix dotted with a column of the second. We want image \(i\) (row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{I}\)) dotted with text vector \(j\). But the text vectors are the rows of \(\mathbf{T}\), not its columns, so we first transpose it, written \(\mathbf{T}^{\top}\), which flips its rows into columns. Then:

\[ \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}\, \mathbf{T}^{\top}, \qquad \text{where each entry is } \mathbf{S}_{i,j} = \mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_j \]

Since all the vectors have length 1, each entry is exactly the cosine similarity between image \(i\) and caption \(j\). For our batch:

\[ \mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.96 & 0 & 0.28 \\ 0.28 & 0.96 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.28 & 0.96 \end{bmatrix} \]

For example, \(\mathbf{S}_{2,1} = \mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 = (0)(0.96) + (1)(0.28) + (0)(0) = 0.28\). That entry measures how well the pizza photo matches the caption "a dog."

Read the matrix. Reading across row 1 answers "which caption fits the dog photo?", and reading down column 1 answers "which image fits 'a dog'?" Where a cell sits also tells you whether it is a right or wrong pairing, and this is what makes contrastive learning so efficient:

  • The diagonal (true pairs): cells \((1,1)\), \((2,2)\), and \((3,3)\) are the real pairings, like the dog photo with "a dog." Training pushes these scores as close to \(+1\) as possible.
  • The off-diagonal (in-batch negatives): every other cell, like the pizza photo with "a dog," is a wrong match. Training pushes these scores toward 0 or lower.

Because every other caption in the batch automatically serves as a distractor for each image, you get hundreds or thousands of wrong examples for free, without labeling any wrong answers by hand.

A real batch works the same way, just bigger. With \(N\) pairs in a \(d_e\)-dimensional space, \(\mathbf{I}\) and \(\mathbf{T}\) each have shape \(N \times d_e\), and the result is an \(N \times N\) matrix:

\[ \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}\, \mathbf{T}^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_N \\ \mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_N \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \mathbf{u}_N \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{u}_N \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{u}_N \cdot \mathbf{v}_N \end{bmatrix} \]

4. Teaching the Space: The InfoNCE Loss

Before training, the projection heads have random weights, so the diagonal scores look no different from the random noise around them. To organize the space, we train the heads with the InfoNCE loss (van den Oord et al., 2018), the contrastive objective used by CLIP (Radford et al., 2021). The goal is to turn the left matrix below into the right one.

The idea is to treat the similarity matrix as a set of multiple-choice questions, the same kind of classification problem you solved with softmax and cross-entropy in the prerequisites:

  • Row by row (image to text): each row is one image. Looking across it, which caption in the batch is the right one? For the dog photo's row, the answer is "a dog."
  • Column by column (text to image): each column is one caption. Looking down it, which image in the batch is the right one? For the "a dog" column, the answer is the dog photo.

How the loss works

You already know the pieces from the prerequisites. For each image, take its row of similarity scores, divide them by a temperature \(\tau\) (more on that below), and run a softmax to turn them into probabilities over the \(N\) captions. The loss for that image is the cross-entropy: the negative log of the probability given to the correct caption. It is small when the model is confident in the right answer and large when it isn't.

InfoNCE does this for every row (image to text) and every column (text to image), and averages the two directions. Minimizing it with gradient descent pulls each matching image and caption together on the unit hypersphere and pushes the non-matching ones apart. You will turn this into code yourself in the assignment.

Why temperature matters

This is the same temperature you met in the Foundations module, doing the same job inside the softmax. Cosine similarities are squeezed between \(-1\) and \(+1\), so the scores in a row are often very close, like \([0.35,\ 0.30,\ 0.28]\). A softmax over scores that close gives nearly equal probabilities, so the model barely sees a winner and learns slowly. Dividing by a small temperature such as \(\tau = 0.07\) (CLIP's starting value) spreads the scores out first, so the correct caption stands out:

CLIP learns \(\tau\) during training along with the other weights.

5. Architectural Trade-Offs: How to Choose

When building a multimodal system, start from what it has to do:

Choose coordinated spaces (CLIP-style) for:

  • Large-scale search. Embed your collection once and search it with a library like Faiss. For example, 50 million product photos with text search that answers in under 15 milliseconds.
  • Zero-shot classification. Label an image without training a classifier: encode prompts like "a photo of a dog" and "a photo of a cat," then pick the prompt whose vector is closest to the image's vector. Adding a new category only means writing a new prompt, with no retraining.
  • Inputs that arrive separately. Modalities show up at different times, or on different devices, and need to be encoded independently.

Choose joint representations (cross-attention or fusion) for:

  • Visual question answering (VQA). Answering detailed questions about an image, such as "what is the person on the left holding?", where words in the question must connect to specific regions of the image.
  • Emotion and tone recognition. Reading emotion from facial expressions and words together, where neither tells the whole story alone.
  • Generative captioning. Writing new text that describes an image.

The best of both: modern vision-language models

You don't always have to pick one. Multimodal large language models (MLLMs), chatbots that accept images, such as LLaVA, combine both strategies:

  1. A pretrained, coordinated image encoder (CLIP's) turns the image into vectors that already line up with language.
  2. A small projection layer resizes those vectors to match the language model's token embeddings, and they are fed into the language model alongside the text tokens. The language model's attention layers then process image and text tokens together, which is a joint stage.

Check Your Understanding

Check 1: sizing projection heads

Question: You want to map one encoder's output (\(\mathbf{z}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{1024}\)) and a text encoder's output (\(\mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{768}\)) into a shared space of size \(d_e = 256\), using single linear layers without biases (\(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}\)). How many trainable weights do these projection heads add?

Solution:

  • The first projection matrix has shape \(256 \times 1024\): \(262{,}144\) weights.
  • The text projection matrix has shape \(256 \times 768\): \(196{,}608\) weights.
  • Total: \(262{,}144 + 196{,}608 = \mathbf{458{,}752}\) weights.

That is less than half a million weights, a tiny fraction of the hundreds of millions inside the encoders themselves, which is why training only the projection heads is cheap.

Check 2: direction vs. length

Question: In a two-dimensional toy space, an image vector is projected to \(\mathbf{p}_v = [3.0,\ 4.0]\). Two candidate captions come out as \(\mathbf{p}_{t1} = [6.0,\ 8.0]\) and \(\mathbf{p}_{t2} = [-4.0,\ 3.0]\). Which caption matches, and why?

Solution: First normalize the image vector:

\[ \Vert \mathbf{p}_v \Vert_2 = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5.0 \implies \mathbf{u} = [0.6,\ 0.8] \]

Caption 1:

\[ \Vert \mathbf{p}_{t1} \Vert_2 = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10.0 \implies \mathbf{v}_1 = [0.6,\ 0.8] \] \[ S_C(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}_1) = (0.6)(0.6) + (0.8)(0.8) = 0.36 + 0.64 = \mathbf{1.0} \]

Caption 2:

\[ \Vert \mathbf{p}_{t2} \Vert_2 = \sqrt{(-4)^2 + 3^2} = 5.0 \implies \mathbf{v}_2 = [-0.8,\ 0.6] \] \[ S_C(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}_2) = (0.6)(-0.8) + (0.8)(0.6) = -0.48 + 0.48 = \mathbf{0.0} \]

Result: Caption 1 is an exact match (1.0): it points in the same direction, even though it was twice as long before normalizing. Caption 2 sits at a 90° angle (0.0), so it is orthogonal and unrelated.

Check 3: the size of three-way tensor fusion

Question: A self-driving car's perception system produces three feature vectors:

  • Lidar bird's-eye view (a top-down map built from laser range measurements): \(\mathbb{R}^{32}\)
  • Front-facing camera: \(\mathbb{R}^{64}\)
  • Radar: \(\mathbb{R}^{16}\)

If you build a full three-way tensor fusion representation, how many entries does the fused vector have?

Solution: Append a 1 to each vector, then multiply the lengths:

\[ d_{\text{fused}} = (32 + 1) \times (64 + 1) \times (16 + 1) = 33 \times 65 \times 17 = \mathbf{36{,}465} \]

Passing this single vector to a dense layer with 256 units would need \(36{,}465 \times 256 \approx 9.33\) million weights just for that first layer.

Core Takeaways

  • The mismatch: separate encoders produce vectors of different lengths whose coordinates mean different things, so they can't be compared directly.
  • Joint vs. coordinated: joint models merge features into one vector for deep reasoning. Coordinated spaces keep vectors separate but place them in one shared space, which makes search and zero-shot classification fast.
  • Projection and normalization: linear projection heads map different sizes into the same size \(d_e\), and \(L_2\) normalization removes length so the model compares direction only.
  • InfoNCE: contrastive training treats the batch similarity matrix as a two-way multiple-choice test, pulling true pairs together and pushing the other pairs in the batch apart.
  • Temperature: dividing similarities by a small \(\tau\) sharpens the softmax, which gives clearer gradient updates during training.
  • Both together: modern multimodal chatbots use a coordinated image encoder followed by a joint language model.
