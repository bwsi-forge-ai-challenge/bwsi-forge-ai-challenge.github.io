Multimodal Representation & Latent Spaces

In the previous lesson, we framed the core challenge of multimodality: different sensors capture fundamentally different phenomena across space and time. Before a system can reason across those differences, it has to convert pixels, audio waveforms, and sentences into numerical vectors.

That introduces an immediate roadblock: vectors emitted by separate encoders cannot be compared out of the box.

This lesson covers how we solve that problem. We will examine the two fundamental architectural paradigms, joint representations and coordinated spaces, and step through the linear algebra and the contrastive training objective that bind them. You will implement the same mechanics in PyTorch in this unit's assignment.

What you should already know. This lesson builds directly on earlier material. If any of these feel rusty, skim them first:

  • Vectors, dot products, and vector length (norm): Vectors
  • Matrix multiplication and transposes: Matrices
  • Embeddings and cosine similarity: Embeddings: Meaning in Vector Space
  • Softmax and cross-entropy loss: Multi-Layer Perceptrons and Classification and Logistic Regression

1. The Dimensional and Semantic Mismatch

Recall from the Embeddings lesson that an embedding is a list of numbers that captures the meaning of an input. The network that produces it is called an encoder. Each modality needs its own encoder, because a sentence and a photo have completely different raw forms.

For text, the encoder is the transformer you built up in the Foundations module. For images, a popular choice is the Vision Transformer (ViT). It cuts an image into a grid of small squares called patches (for example, 16 × 16 pixels each), turns each patch into a vector, and treats those vectors exactly like word tokens. The same attention layers you studied for text then run over the patches.

So when you pass an image into a ViT and a sentence into a text encoder, each model emits an embedding. But you cannot simply compute their dot product or measure the distance between them. Two structural walls prevent it:

Notation: \(\mathbb{R}^{768}\) ("R to the 768") is shorthand for "a list of 768 real numbers." A vector \(\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{768}\) is simply a vector with 768 entries.

  • Dimensional incompatibility. The two encoders are built and trained independently, so nothing forces their outputs to be the same size. In the base CLIP model (a well-known image-and-text model from OpenAI), the vision encoder outputs vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{768}\), while the text encoder outputs vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{512}\). A dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them up, so it is undefined when one vector has 768 entries and the other has 512.
  • Semantic basis disconnect. Even if you forced both encoders to emit vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{512}\), coordinate 42 in the vision space might encode something like the orientation of edges, while coordinate 42 in the language space encodes something like verb tense. Each encoder learned its own meaning for each coordinate while being trained on its own task, so the coordinates do not line up.

A multimodal latent space is a learned vector space built specifically to fix these mismatches. ("Latent" just means hidden: these are the model's internal vectors, not the raw input.) There are two ways to structure that space:

Zero-shot transfer means handling a task the model was never trained on, such as recognizing a category it never saw labeled examples of. You will see exactly how a coordinated space makes this possible in Section 5.

2. Strategy A: Joint Representations

Joint architectures compress all modalities into a single combined vector:

\[ \mathbf{z}_{\text{joint}} = f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{image}},\ \mathbf{x}_{\text{audio}},\ \mathbf{x}_{\text{text}}) \]

Here \(f\) is whatever combining operation we choose. The fused vector then goes into a prediction head: a small network on top, like the output layer of an MLP, that makes the final prediction, such as a class label or a number.

Early concatenation

The simplest joint representation normalizes each feature vector and stacks them end to end. The symbol \(\Vert\) means "glue these vectors together":

\[ \mathbf{z}_{\text{concat}} = \left[\, \mathbf{z}_a \,\Vert\, \mathbf{z}_v \,\Vert\, \mathbf{z}_t \,\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a + d_v + d_t} \]

Each \(d\) is the length of that modality's vector. If an audio vector is in \(\mathbb{R}^{64}\), vision is in \(\mathbb{R}^{128}\), and text is in \(\mathbb{R}^{64}\), the concatenated vector has \(64 + 128 + 64 = 256\) entries.

Concatenation is cheap, but on its own it does not model how the modalities interact. It hands the entire job of discovering interactions to the layers that come after it. Those layers often take a shortcut and rely mostly on whichever modality is easiest to learn from, while the others are ignored. This failure mode is called modality collapse.

Tensor fusion: modeling multiplicative interactions

Some meaning only appears when modalities are combined. A speaker saying "Brilliant idea" can be earnest or sarcastic depending on whether their voice drops flat or their eyes roll. Neither the words nor the tone alone tells you which.

Tensor fusion (Zadeh et al., 2017) captures these combinations explicitly using the outer product. Where a dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them into one number, an outer product multiplies every entry of one vector by every entry of the other and keeps all the results in a grid. Before taking it, a 1 is appended to each vector:

\[ \mathbf{z}_{\text{tensor}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{z}_v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \otimes \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{z}_t \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Let's trace a small example where \(\mathbf{z}_v = [v_1, v_2]\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t = [t_1, t_2]\). Appending a 1 to each gives vectors of length 3, and their outer product is a 3 × 3 grid:

\[ \mathbf{z}_{\text{tensor}} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} t_1 & t_2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 t_1 & v_1 t_2 & v_1 \\ v_2 t_1 & v_2 t_2 & v_2 \\ t_1 & t_2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]
\(t_1\)\(t_2\)1
\(v_1\)\(v_1 \cdot t_1\)\(v_1 \cdot t_2\)\(v_1\) (visual)
\(v_2\)\(v_2 \cdot t_1\)\(v_2 \cdot t_2\)\(v_2\) (visual)
1\(t_1\) (text)\(t_2\) (text)1 (bias)

The shaded block holds the bimodal cross-terms.

Notice what this grid contains:

  • The top-left 2 × 2 block captures the bimodal interactions, every product \(v_i t_j\) of a visual feature and a text feature.
  • The rightmost column preserves the original visual values \(v_1, v_2\).
  • The bottom row preserves the original text values \(t_1, t_2\).
  • The 1 in the corner acts as a constant bias term. Appending the 1s is what lets the grid keep each modality's original values alongside the products.

The grid is then flattened into one long vector and passed to the prediction head. With three modalities, the outer product becomes a 3-D block of numbers rather than a grid, and it also contains every three-way (trimodal) product.

The scalability bottleneck

The size of an outer product is the product of the vector lengths (each plus 1), so it grows exponentially with the number of modalities \(M\). The symbol \(\prod\) means "multiply all of these together":

\[ d_{\text{tensor}} = \prod_{m=1}^{M} (d_m + 1) \]

For audio, vision, and text vectors of 64 entries each, the flattened tensor has \(65 \times 65 \times 65 = \mathbf{274{,}625}\) entries. A single linear layer mapping it to 128 hidden units would need \(274{,}625 \times 128 \approx 35\) million weights, which quickly causes memory bottlenecks and overfitting.

Modern architectures avoid this with Low-Rank Multimodal Fusion, which approximates the big tensor with much smaller pieces, or with cross-attention layers, which you will meet in the next lesson.

3. Strategy B: Coordinated Spaces

Coordinated representations take the opposite approach: keep the modalities in separate vectors, but teach them a common geometric language, so that an image and a caption describing it end up as nearby vectors.

The big payoff is retrieval, meaning search. Because each image is encoded on its own, you can encode 100 million photos once, ahead of time, and store the vectors in an index. When a user types a text query, you encode just that query and find the stored image vectors with the highest dot product with it. Libraries such as Facebook AI's Faiss are built to do this search over millions of vectors in milliseconds, without comparing against every single one. You never have to run an expensive joint model on every possible image–text pair.

Building a coordinated space takes three components.

1. Projection heads

To reconcile the mismatched output sizes, we attach a small learnable layer called a projection head to each encoder. It is usually a single linear layer (a weight matrix plus a bias vector, the same building block as one layer of an MLP), or a small two-layer MLP:

\[ \mathbf{p}_v = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{z}_v + \mathbf{b}_v \quad \text{where } \mathbf{W}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times d_v} \] \[ \mathbf{p}_t = \mathbf{W}_t \mathbf{z}_t + \mathbf{b}_t \quad \text{where } \mathbf{W}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times d_t} \]

Here \(d_e\) is the size of the shared space we are aiming for. A \(d_e \times d_v\) matrix times a vector of length \(d_v\) gives a vector of length \(d_e\), so both outputs end up the same size. If the image encoder outputs \(d_v = 768\), the text encoder outputs \(d_t = 512\), and the shared space is \(d_e = 512\):

  • \(\mathbf{W}_v\) has shape \(512 \times 768\), which is \(393{,}216\) weights.
  • \(\mathbf{W}_t\) has shape \(512 \times 512\), which is \(262{,}144\) weights.

Matching the sizes fixes the first wall. Fixing the second wall, the unaligned meanings, comes from training these heads so that each coordinate of the shared space comes to mean the same thing for both modalities. Section 4 shows how.

2. Cosine similarity and directional geometry

The length of an embedding often reflects things we don't care about, such as how bright an image is or how long a sentence is. To compare meaning regardless of length, we compare only directions. First, each vector is divided by its own length (its \(L_2\) norm, from the Vectors lesson), which is called \(L_2\) normalization:

\[ \mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{p}_v}{\Vert \mathbf{p}_v \Vert_2}, \qquad \mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{p}_t}{\Vert \mathbf{p}_t \Vert_2} \]

After this, every vector has length exactly 1. In two dimensions, all the vectors' tips lie on a circle of radius 1; in many dimensions, they lie on the surface of a sphere of radius 1, called the unit hypersphere. Now only direction matters.

Once vectors have length 1, the cosine similarity you met in the Embeddings lesson simplifies to a plain dot product, because the denominator becomes \(1 \times 1\):

\[ S_C(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\Vert \mathbf{u} \Vert_2 \, \Vert \mathbf{v} \Vert_2} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \sum_{k=1}^{d_e} u_k v_k \]

Here \(\theta\) is the angle between the two vectors. The score always lies between \(-1\) and \(+1\):

  • \(+1\): the vectors point the same way (the same meaning).
  • \(0\): the vectors are at right angles, or orthogonal (unrelated meanings).
  • \(-1\): the vectors point in opposite directions.

3. The batch similarity matrix

Models are trained on a mini-batch: a small group of examples processed together, here \(N\) images and their \(N\) matching captions. Stack the normalized image vectors as the rows of a matrix \(\mathbf{I}\), with \(N\) rows and \(d_e\) columns, and the normalized text vectors as the rows of \(\mathbf{T}\). A single matrix multiplication (from the Matrices lesson) then computes every image–caption similarity at once:

\[ \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}\, \mathbf{T}^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N} \] \[ \mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_N \\ \mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_N \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \mathbf{u}_N \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{u}_N \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{u}_N \cdot \mathbf{v}_N \end{bmatrix} \]

Entry \(\mathbf{S}_{i,j}\) is the similarity between image \(i\) and caption \(j\). The diagonal entries \(\mathbf{S}_{i,i}\) are the true pairs: each image with its own caption. The off-diagonal entries \(\mathbf{S}_{i,j}\) with \(i \neq j\) are wrong pairings, called in-batch negatives. They come for free: every other caption in the batch serves as a wrong answer.

4. Training Coordinated Spaces: InfoNCE Loss

Before training, the projection heads have random weights, so the diagonal and off-diagonal similarities look like the same random noise. To align the space, we train the heads with the InfoNCE objective (van den Oord et al., 2018), the loss used by CLIP (Radford et al., 2021).

The key idea is to treat the similarity matrix as a set of multiple-choice questions, the same kind of classification problem you solved with softmax and cross-entropy in the prerequisites:

  • Row by row (image to text): for image \(i\), which of the \(N\) captions in the batch is the correct one?
  • Column by column (text to image): for caption \(j\), which of the \(N\) images in the batch is the correct one?

The mathematical formulation

For image \(i\), we turn its row of similarities into probabilities with the softmax function, dividing each score by a temperature \(\tau\) first (more on that below):

\[ P(\text{caption } i \mid \text{image } i) = \frac{\exp\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_i}{\tau}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_j}{\tau}\right)} \]

The numerator is the score of the correct caption, and the denominator adds up the scores of all \(N\) captions, so the result is between 0 and 1. The loss is the cross-entropy: the negative log of the probability given to the correct answer, averaged over all \(N\) images. It is small when the model puts high probability on the true caption and large when it does not:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{i \to t} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\exp\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_i}{\tau}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_j}{\tau}\right)} \]

We compute the matching loss \(\mathcal{L}_{t \to i}\) for the text-to-image direction (working down the columns instead of across the rows) and average the two:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \mathcal{L}_{i \to t} + \mathcal{L}_{t \to i} \right) \]

Minimizing this symmetric loss with gradient descent pulls each matching pair together on the unit hypersphere while pushing the non-matching pairs apart.

The role of temperature

You met temperature in the Foundations module as the knob that makes text generation more or less random. Here it plays the same role inside the softmax. Because cosine similarities lie between \(-1\) and \(+1\), the differences between them are small, and a softmax over small differences gives a flat, uncertain distribution. Dividing by \(\tau\) scales the scores up before the softmax. The scaled scores are called logits:

\[ \text{logit} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\tau} \]

Worked example. One image's row has three candidate captions with cosine scores \([0.40,\ 0.25,\ 0.20]\), where the first is the correct pair.

When \(\tau = 1.0\) (no scaling):

\[ \text{logits} = [0.40,\ 0.25,\ 0.20] \implies \text{softmax} = [0.373,\ 0.321,\ 0.306] \]

The model barely distinguishes the true pair from the wrong ones, so the training signal (the gradient) is weak.

When \(\tau = 0.07\) (CLIP's starting value):

\[ \text{logits} = [5.71,\ 3.57,\ 2.86] \implies \text{softmax} = [0.851,\ 0.100,\ 0.049] \]

The same similarities now produce a sharp distribution centered on the true match, which gives a much more informative gradient.

In practice, CLIP learns \(\tau\) during training along with the other weights. It stores the temperature as a logarithm so it always stays positive, and caps how far the logits can be scaled to keep training stable.

Negative sampling dynamics

The quality of a coordinated space depends heavily on the batch size \(N\), because \(N\) sets how many wrong answers each example must beat.

If \(N = 4\), the model only has to tell a dog photo apart from three unrelated captions, say about an airplane, a submarine, and an invoice. That is easy, so it learns little. If \(N = 32{,}768\), as in CLIP, the batch will contain hundreds of similar distractors: golden retrievers, labradors, wolves, foxes. To pick the right caption out of tens of thousands of close alternatives, the model is forced to learn fine-grained details.

In code

The whole objective is only a few lines of PyTorch. You will write and train it yourself in the assignment.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

u = F.normalize(head_img(z_img), dim=1)   # N x d_e image vectors, each of length 1
v = F.normalize(head_txt(z_txt), dim=1)   # N x d_e text vectors, each of length 1
S = u @ v.T                                # N x N similarity matrix
logits = S / tau
targets = torch.arange(N)                  # the right caption for image i is caption i
loss = (F.cross_entropy(logits, targets) + F.cross_entropy(logits.T, targets)) / 2

5. Architectural Trade-Offs

When designing a multimodal system, start from what the system has to do:

Choose coordinated spaces (CLIP-style) for:

  • Large-scale search. You have 50 million product photos and need text-based search that answers in under 15 milliseconds.
  • Zero-shot classification. To label an image without training a classifier, encode prompts like "a photo of a dog," "a photo of a cat," and so on, and pick the one whose vector is closest to the image's vector. Adding a new category only means writing a new prompt.
  • Inputs that arrive separately. Modalities arrive at different times, or on different devices, and need to be encoded independently.

Choose joint representations (cross-attention or fusion) for:

  • Visual question answering (VQA). Answering detailed questions about an image, such as "what is the person on the left holding?", where words in the question must connect to specific regions of the image.
  • Multimodal affect recognition. Reading emotion from synchronized video frames, voice pitch, and words, where no single modality tells the whole story.
  • Generative captioning. Writing new text that describes an image.

Modern multimodal large language models (MLLMs), chatbots that accept images, often combine both. In LLaVA, for example, a coordinated vision encoder (a CLIP ViT) turns image patches into vectors that are already aligned with language, and those vectors are then fed to a language model that reasons over image and text tokens together, a joint stage.

Check your understanding: the dimensionality of multi-source tensor fusion

Question: A self-driving car's perception system produces three feature vectors:

  • Lidar bird's-eye view (a top-down map built from laser range measurements): \(\mathbb{R}^{32}\)
  • Front-facing camera: \(\mathbb{R}^{64}\)
  • Radar point cloud: \(\mathbb{R}^{16}\)

If you build a full three-way tensor fusion representation, how many entries does the fused vector have?

Solution: Append a 1 to each vector, then multiply the lengths:

\[ d_{\text{fused}} = (32 + 1) \times (64 + 1) \times (16 + 1) = 33 \times 65 \times 17 = \mathbf{36{,}465} \]

Passing this single vector to a dense layer with 256 units would need \(36{,}465 \times 256 \approx 9.33\) million weights just for that first layer.

Check your understanding: angles in a coordinated space

Question: In a two-dimensional toy space, an image vector is \(\mathbf{p}_v = [3.0,\ 4.0]\). Two candidate caption vectors are \(\mathbf{p}_{t1} = [6.0,\ 8.0]\) and \(\mathbf{p}_{t2} = [-4.0,\ 3.0]\). Compute the cosine similarity for both captions. Which caption matches, and why?

Solution: First normalize the image vector:

\[ \Vert \mathbf{p}_v \Vert_2 = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5.0 \implies \mathbf{u} = [0.6,\ 0.8] \]

Candidate 1:

\[ \Vert \mathbf{p}_{t1} \Vert_2 = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10.0 \implies \mathbf{v}_1 = [0.6,\ 0.8] \] \[ S_C(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}_1) = (0.6)(0.6) + (0.8)(0.8) = 0.36 + 0.64 = \mathbf{1.0} \]

Candidate 2:

\[ \Vert \mathbf{p}_{t2} \Vert_2 = \sqrt{(-4)^2 + 3^2} = 5.0 \implies \mathbf{v}_2 = [-0.8,\ 0.6] \] \[ S_C(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}_2) = (0.6)(-0.8) + (0.8)(0.6) = -0.48 + 0.48 = \mathbf{0.0} \]

Result: Caption 1 matches with a cosine similarity of 1.0: it points in exactly the same direction, even though it is twice as long. Caption 2 is orthogonal (\(S_C = 0.0\)), so it is unrelated.

Check your understanding: sizing projection heads

Question: You want to map an audio encoder's output (\(\mathbf{z}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{1024}\)) and a text encoder's output (\(\mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{768}\)) into a shared space of size \(d_e = 256\), using single linear layers without biases. How many trainable weights do these projection heads add?

Solution: The audio projection matrix has shape \(256 \times 1024\):

\[ 256 \times 1024 = 262{,}144 \text{ weights} \]

The text projection matrix has shape \(256 \times 768\):

\[ 256 \times 768 = 196{,}608 \text{ weights} \]

In total:

\[ 262{,}144 + 196{,}608 = \mathbf{458{,}752} \text{ weights} \]

That is tiny next to the hundreds of millions of weights in the encoders themselves, which is why training only the projection heads is cheap.

Key Takeaways

  • Separate encoders produce vectors of different sizes whose coordinates mean different things, so they cannot be compared directly.
  • Joint representations fuse modalities into one vector. Concatenation is cheap but leaves interactions to later layers; tensor fusion captures every interaction but grows exponentially with the number of modalities.
  • Coordinated spaces keep modalities separate: projection heads map each encoder into a shared space, \(L_2\) normalization puts every vector on the unit hypersphere, and cosine similarity compares directions.
  • The InfoNCE loss treats each row and column of the batch similarity matrix as a classification problem, pulling true pairs together and pushing in-batch negatives apart. A low temperature sharpens the softmax, and large batches supply harder negatives.
  • Use coordinated spaces for search and zero-shot classification, and joint representations for reasoning that combines modalities. Modern multimodal chatbots use both.
