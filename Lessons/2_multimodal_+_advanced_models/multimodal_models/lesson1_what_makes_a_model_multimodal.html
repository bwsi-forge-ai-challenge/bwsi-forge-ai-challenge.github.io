What Makes a Model Multimodal?

Every model in the Foundations module read and wrote text. People, though, see, hear, and speak. This lesson shows how a model can work with images and sound by turning them into the same thing it already understands: sequences of vectors.

1. Modalities Are Different Shapes of Data

A modality is a kind of input or output: text, images, audio, video, and so on. A multimodal model takes in or produces more than one modality, for example a model that answers questions about a photo, or one that turns a written description into music.

The challenge is that each modality arrives as a very different shape of data:

ModalityRaw formExample shapeWhat one number means
TextA sequence of token IDs[12] for a 12-token sentenceWhich word piece (Foundations, Lesson 1)
ImageA grid of pixels with 3 color channels[3 × 224 × 224] = 150,528 numbersHow red, green, or blue one dot is
AudioA sequence of air-pressure samples[160,000] for 10 seconds at 16 kHzAir pressure at one instant

A single word token already carries meaning. A single pixel or audio sample does not: one red dot tells you almost nothing, and the same is true of one instant of sound. Meaning in images and audio lives in patterns across thousands of numbers. So the first job of a multimodal model is to turn those raw numbers into meaningful units.

2. The Common Move: Turn Everything Into Tokens

Recall the pipeline from Transformers: Putting It All Together. Once text is a sequence of embedding vectors, the transformer blocks do not care where those vectors came from. Multimodal models exploit this. Each modality gets its own encoder, a network that turns raw data into a sequence of embedding vectors, which the transformer then processes like word tokens.

Images become patches

The Vision Transformer (ViT) cuts an image into a grid of small squares called patches, typically 16 × 16 pixels. A 224 × 224 image becomes a 14 × 14 grid, which is 196 patches. Each patch is flattened and multiplied by a learned matrix to produce one embedding vector, exactly like looking up a token's embedding. A position vector is added so the model knows where each patch sat in the image, just as position vectors tell a language model the order of words.

Image [3 × 224 × 224] → 196 patches → [196 × d] patch embeddings

Audio becomes spectrogram frames

Raw audio is far too long to feed in directly. Ten seconds at 16,000 samples per second is 160,000 numbers. Speech models such as OpenAI's Whisper first convert the sound into a spectrogram, a picture of which frequencies are present at each moment, and then treat short slices of it as tokens. Whisper reads 30-second chunks as an 80-band log-Mel spectrogram and turns each chunk into 1,500 embedding vectors. The audio deep dive later in this module explains spectrograms in detail.

Audio [480,000] samples (30 s) → spectrogram [80 × 3,000] → [1,500 × d] audio embeddings

After this step, a sentence, a photo, and a voice clip are all the same kind of object: a sequence of vectors with shape [number of tokens × d]. Everything you learned about attention now applies to all three.

Check your understanding: patches

Question: A ViT uses 16 × 16 patches on a 384 × 384 image. How many patch tokens does it produce?

Answer: 384 ÷ 16 = 24 patches per side, so 24 × 24 = 576 tokens. Higher-resolution images cost more tokens, just as longer text does.

3. A Shared Space for Meaning

Encoders turn each modality into vectors, but an image encoder and a text encoder trained separately would place their vectors in unrelated spaces. A picture of a dog and the word "dog" would have no reason to land near each other. To connect modalities, we want a shared embedding space: one space where the image of a dog and the caption "a photo of a dog" end up close together.

You already know this idea from Embeddings, where words with similar meanings sit near each other. A shared space extends it across modalities.

CLIP: learning a shared space from captions

OpenAI's CLIP (2021) trains an image encoder and a text encoder together on about 400 million image–caption pairs collected from the internet. The training method is called contrastive learning:

  1. Take a batch of \(N\) images and their \(N\) captions.
  2. Encode all of them and compute the cosine similarity between every image and every caption, giving an \(N \times N\) grid of scores.
  3. Train both encoders so the \(N\) correct pairs, on the diagonal of the grid, score high and every mismatched pair scores low.
\[ \text{similarity}(\text{image}, \text{text}) = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_{\text{image}} \cdot \mathbf{v}_{\text{text}}}{\lVert \mathbf{v}_{\text{image}} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{v}_{\text{text}} \rVert} \]

No one labels the images with categories. The captions people already wrote on the web are the supervision. The result is a space where you can compare any image with any piece of text by measuring how close their vectors are.

Try it: a contrastive similarity grid. Each row is an image and each column is a caption. Click a caption to see how well it matches each image. Training pushes the diagonal up and everything else down.

Similarity values are illustrative, chosen to look like a trained CLIP model.

What a shared space makes possible

  • Zero-shot classification. To classify a photo without any training, embed captions such as "a photo of a cat" and "a photo of a dog", and pick the one closest to the image.
  • Search across modalities. Type a text query and retrieve the closest images, or the reverse.
  • Measuring how well an image matches text. The similarity score itself is a useful metric. You will use exactly this in Project 1 to measure how much meaning survives each step of a chain.
  • Guiding generators. Text-to-image models use text encoders, often from CLIP, to steer what they draw.

The same recipe works for other pairs of modalities. CLAP learns a shared space for audio and text, and Meta's ImageBind links six modalities, including images, text, and audio, into one space by pairing each with images.

Check your understanding: the training grid

Question: A CLIP training batch has 32,768 image–caption pairs. How many similarity scores are in the grid, and how many of them should training push up?

Answer: 32,768 × 32,768, about 1.07 billion scores. Only the 32,768 on the diagonal, the true pairs, are pushed up; all the others are pushed down. Large batches give the model many hard negatives to learn from.

4. Three Ways Models Combine Modalities

Almost every multimodal system you will meet is built in one of three ways. They reuse the encoders and the transformer you already know, and differ in where the modalities meet.

DesignHow it worksGood forExamples
Shared embedding spaceSeparate encoders, trained so matching inputs land close togetherSearch, classification, similarity scoresCLIP, CLAP, ImageBind
Encoder + language modelAn image or audio encoder produces vectors; a small learned projection maps them into the language model's token space, and the language model reads them like extra wordsDescribing images, answering questions about them, transcribing speechLLaVA, Whisper
Conditioned generatorA generator for one modality is steered by embeddings of another through cross-attentionMaking images, music, or speech from a text promptStable Diffusion, MusicGen

The middle row is worth a closer look, because it is how most chatbots "see". LLaVA, for example, passes an image through CLIP's image encoder, multiplies each patch vector by a learned matrix so it has the same size as a word embedding, and places those vectors in front of the text prompt. The language model then attends to image patches and words together. To the transformer, the picture is just a few hundred extra tokens.

The bottom row is the subject of the two deep dives in this module: the audio deep dive shows how MusicGen turns text into sound, and the image deep dive shows how diffusion models turn text into pictures.

Key Takeaways

  • Each modality has its own raw shape. An encoder turns it into a sequence of embedding vectors so a transformer can process it.
  • Images become patch tokens; audio becomes spectrogram frames.
  • Contrastive learning, as in CLIP, builds a shared embedding space where matching images and text land close together. Cosine similarity in that space measures how well two things match.
  • Multimodal systems combine modalities through a shared space, an encoder feeding a language model, or a generator conditioned on another modality.
