Chaining Models and What Gets Lost

Many AI systems use several models connected in a row. One model describes an image, another turns the description into speech, a third transcribes that speech, and another generates a new image. Each model handles one task, but information can disappear or change as it moves through the chain.

In this lesson, we'll compare these pipelines with models that handle multiple types of data at once. We'll also look at why errors build up, how to measure what survives, and how to test these ideas in Project 1.

1. Pipelines vs. Native Multimodal Models

Imagine building a chatbot that can listen to you and respond out loud. There are two main ways to approach this.

Option 1: Connect several models in a pipeline.

Each model handles one step:

  1. A speech recognition model turns your voice into text.
  2. A language model reads the text and writes a response.
  3. A text-to-speech model turns that response into audio.

This was how ChatGPT's Voice Mode worked before May 2024. Each model passed its output to the next one.

The problem is that converting everything to text can throw away useful information. A transcript might capture the words you said, but not your tone of voice, background noises, or whether several people were speaking. The system also has to wait for one model to finish before the next can begin, which can make the conversation feel slow.

Option 2: Use a natively multimodal model.

A natively multimodal model handles multiple types of data within a single network. For example, OpenAI's GPT-4o was trained across text, images, and audio. Rather than relying entirely on a transcript, it can process audio directly and respond with audio.

OpenAI reported that GPT-4o's audio responses averaged about 320 milliseconds of latency—roughly the length of a natural pause in conversation.

Here's how the two approaches compare:

Pipeline of modelsNative multimodal model
InformationDetails may be lost when data is converted between formats, especially through textCan preserve richer information in internal representations
SpeedDelays from each step add upCan process and respond in a more integrated way
DebuggingEach model can be tested or replaced separatelyHarder to inspect and debug because everything is part of one large model
RequirementsCan combine existing models that are available to downloadUsually requires much more paired multimodal training data and computing power

Pipelines are still widely used because they're modular, practical, and often inexpensive to build. In Project 1, you'll deliberately build one so you can investigate where information gets lost.

2. Errors Compound Along a Chain

In the previous lesson, we saw that converting between modalities can either remove information or introduce details that weren't in the original. When you connect several models, these effects can accumulate.

Suppose a model describes a photo but leaves out the red frisbee in the picture. A speech model can read that description perfectly, and a transcription model can accurately recover the words. But when the final model generates a new image, the frisbee is still missing. The later models cannot recover a detail that disappeared earlier unless they have some other way to infer it.

We can use a simple mathematical example to understand how this adds up.

Suppose every step preserves 90% of the meaning it receives. After \(n\) steps, the fraction of the original meaning that remains is approximately:

\[ \text{Meaning retained after } n \text{ steps} = 0.9^{\,n} \]

That gives us:

  • After 1 step: 90% remains.
  • After 3 steps: about 73% remains.
  • After 5 steps: about 59% remains.

This is a simplified model, not a rule that every AI system follows. Some steps preserve nearly all the information, while others lose much more. Describing a complicated photo, for example, may lose more detail than transcribing clear speech. The weakest steps can have the biggest effect on the final result.

Why chains drift

Think of the game of telephone: one person whispers a message to the next, and the message gradually changes as it travels down the line.

The changes aren't always random. When someone mishears a word, they may replace it with something that sounds more familiar. AI models can behave similarly. When information is missing or ambiguous, a model may fill in the gaps with details that are common in its training data.

As a result, a chain can gradually lose specific details and drift toward more generic descriptions. If one model introduces a mistake, later models may treat that mistake as if it were correct.

Check your understanding: compounding loss

Imagine a pipeline with four steps:

  • Image captioning preserves 70% of the meaning.
  • Text-to-speech preserves 99%.
  • Speech recognition preserves 95%.
  • Image generation preserves 80%.

Question: Roughly how much of the original meaning survives one complete pass through the pipeline? Which step should you improve first?

Answer: Multiply the retention rates:

\[ 0.70 \times 0.99 \times 0.95 \times 0.80 \approx 0.53 \]

About 53% remains in this simplified example. The captioning step is the weakest link, preserving only 70% of its input. Improving the captioner is a good place to start because it can preserve more of the original details for every later model to use.

3. Measuring What Survives

Saying that an image or message "changed a lot" is a useful observation, but it isn't enough to compare different models or pipelines. To do that, we need measurements.

Different metrics measure different kinds of similarity. No single score tells us everything about whether the original meaning survived.

Word error rate: Did the words survive?

Word error rate (WER) measures how different a transcript is from the original text.

To calculate it, compare the two sequences of words and count the smallest number of changes needed to turn one into the other. There are three types of changes:

  • Substitution: One word is replaced by another.
  • Deletion: A word is missing.
  • Insertion: An extra word appears.

Add up these changes and divide by the number of words in the original text:

\[ \text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N} \]

Here, \(S\) is the number of substitutions, \(D\) is the number of deletions, \(I\) is the number of insertions, and \(N\) is the number of words in the original.

Example

Original: a brown dog running through a field

Transcript: a brown dog running through the field

The original contains seven words. One word has changed: "a" became "the." So:

\[ \text{WER} = \frac{1}{7} \approx 0.14 \]

A WER of 0 means the transcript matches the original exactly. Lower scores are generally better. WER can sometimes exceed 1 if a transcript contains many insertions.

One limitation: WER measures word differences, not meaning. A transcript can use different words and still communicate the same idea.

Text similarity: Did the meaning survive?

Consider these two descriptions:

  • "A puppy on the sand"
  • "A small dog on a beach"

The words differ, but the meaning is very similar. WER would count several differences, so it isn't the best tool for comparing these sentences.

Instead, we can use a sentence embedding model. It converts each sentence into a numerical representation, called an embedding, that captures aspects of its meaning. We then calculate the cosine similarity between the two embeddings.

A score closer to 1 generally indicates that the sentences are more similar in meaning. This is useful when the wording changes but the underlying idea stays the same.

CLIP similarity: Did the picture survive?

CLIP, introduced in an earlier lesson, gives us another way to compare images and text.

There are two useful comparisons:

  • Image–image similarity: Compare the embedding of a generated image with the embedding of the original. This helps measure how much the image has changed as it passes through a chain.
  • Image–text similarity: Compare an image with a caption. This helps measure how well the caption matches the picture, or how well a generated image matches a text description.

Here's a summary of which metric to use:

What you're comparingMetricWhat a good result looks like
Original text and transcriptWord error rate (WER)Close to 0
Two text descriptionsSentence-embedding cosine similarityClose to 1
Image and captionCLIP image–text similarityHigher than the score for unrelated captions
Generated image and original imageCLIP image–image similarityClose to 1

Important: CLIP image–text and image–image scores are not on the same scale. An image–text score around 0.3 can represent a strong match, depending on the setup. Always compare scores from the same type of measurement rather than comparing the two types directly.

These metrics each capture only part of the picture. A transcript can have a low WER but miss an important detail, and two images can have similar embeddings while differing in ways that matter for your task. Use the metric that matches what you want to measure.

Key Takeaways

  • A pipeline connects several single-purpose models. A native multimodal model handles multiple modalities within one network.
  • Pipelines are modular and practical, but every conversion can lose information and add latency.
  • Errors can compound across a chain. Once a detail disappears, later models may not recover it, and invented details can be passed along as if they were true.
  • Use WER to measure differences between transcripts, sentence-embedding similarity to compare the meaning of text, and CLIP similarity to compare images and image–text pairs.
  • No single metric captures every kind of information loss. Choose measurements that match the question you're trying to answer.
