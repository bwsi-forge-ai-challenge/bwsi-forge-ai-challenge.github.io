Chaining Models and What Gets Lost

Real multimodal products often connect several single-purpose models in a row. This lesson compares that approach with natively multimodal models, shows how errors build up along a chain, and introduces the metrics you will use in Project 1 to measure how much meaning survives.

1. Pipelines vs. Native Multimodal Models

There are two ways to build a system that, for example, lets you talk to a chatbot out loud.

A pipeline chains separate models, each handling one conversion. Before May 2024, ChatGPT's Voice Mode worked this way: one model transcribed your speech to text, GPT-4 read the text and wrote a reply, and a third model turned the reply into speech. OpenAI described the cost of this design: because the language model only ever saw a transcript, it "can't directly observe tone, multiple speakers, or background noises, and it can't output laughter, singing, or express emotion." Responses also took several seconds, because each model had to finish before the next could start.

A natively multimodal model takes in and produces several modalities in one network. OpenAI's GPT-4o was trained end to end across text, images, and audio, so the same model hears the tone of your voice and can respond in speech directly. OpenAI reported audio response times averaging about 320 milliseconds, similar to a human pause in conversation.

Pipelines are still everywhere because they are cheap, modular, and built from models anyone can download. In Project 1 you will build a pipeline on purpose, so you can see exactly where information is lost.

2. Errors Compound Along a Chain

In the last lesson, each conversion either compressed the input or invented details. In a chain, those effects stack up. A detail dropped at step 2 can never be recovered at step 3, and a detail invented at step 3 is treated as fact by step 4.

A simple model shows why chains degrade quickly. Suppose each step keeps 90% of the meaning of its input. After \(n\) steps, the fraction that survives is

\[ \text{kept after } n \text{ steps} = 0.9^{\,n} \]

That is 90% after one step, 73% after three, and only 59% after five. Real steps are not this uniform; speech recognition on clear audio keeps nearly everything, while captioning a busy photo keeps much less. The weakest links dominate.

This is the children's game of telephone: a message whispered down a line of people comes out the other end changed, often in funny and revealing ways. The changes are not random. Each person replaces what they misheard with something that sounds familiar to them. A model in a chain does the same thing, replacing missing details with whatever is most typical in its training data, so chains drift toward generic, common content.

Check your understanding: compounding loss

Question: One round of a chain has four steps: captioning keeps 70% of the meaning, text-to-speech keeps 99%, speech recognition keeps 95%, and image generation keeps 80%. Roughly how much of the original survives one round, and which step should you improve first?

Answer: 0.70 × 0.99 × 0.95 × 0.80 ≈ 0.53, so about half. Captioning is the weakest link, so a better or more detailed captioner would help most.

3. Measuring What Survives

"The picture changed a lot" is not a measurement. To compare chains, models, or settings, you need numbers. Each kind of comparison has a standard metric.

Word error rate: did the words survive?

Word error rate (WER) measures how far a transcript is from the original text. Line up the two word sequences and count the fewest substitutions, deletions, and insertions needed to turn one into the other, then divide by the number of words \(N\) in the original:

\[ \text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N} \]

Worked example. Original: a brown dog running through a field (7 words). Transcript: a brown dog running through the field. One substitution ("a" → "the"), so WER = 1 / 7 ≈ 0.14. A WER of 0 is a perfect transcript; lower is better.

Text similarity: did the meaning survive?

WER only counts exact words, so "a puppy on the sand" and "a small dog on a beach" score badly even though they mean nearly the same thing. To compare meaning, embed both sentences with a sentence embedding model and take the cosine similarity, exactly as in the Embeddings lesson. Scores near 1 mean the same meaning.

CLIP similarity: did the picture survive?

Use the CLIP encoders from Lesson 1. Image–image similarity compares the embedding of a generated image with the original image, which tracks how far a chain has drifted visually. Image–text similarity checks how well an image matches a caption, which tests a single captioning or drawing step.

Key Takeaways

  • A pipeline chains single-purpose models; a native multimodal model handles several modalities in one network. Pipelines are modular and easy to build but lose information and add latency at every step.
  • Losses compound along a chain, and chains drift toward the most typical content in the models' training data.
  • Measure what survives with WER for transcripts, sentence-embedding similarity for meaning, and CLIP similarity for images.
