Crossing Between Modalities

The last lesson showed how images, text, and audio all become vectors. This lesson maps every way to convert one modality into another, the kind of model that handles each direction, and why some directions lose information while others have to invent it.

1. The Conversion Map

With three modalities, image, text, and audio, there are six one-way conversions. Each one is a named task with its own family of models. Text sits in the middle of the map because it is the best-supported modality: almost every model can read or write it.

Explore the map. Pick a direction to see the task, how models do it, and open models you can run yourself.

2. How Each Direction Works

Every conversion reuses pieces you have already met. What changes is which modality the encoder reads and which modality the decoder writes.

Image → text: captioning

A vision encoder turns the image into patch vectors, and a text decoder generates a caption one token at a time while attending to those patches through cross-attention. This is the encoder-decoder transformer from the Foundations module, with an image encoder in place of a text encoder. Small captioning models such as BLIP write one short sentence. Larger vision-language models (VLMs) such as SmolVLM or Qwen2-VL can follow instructions like "describe this image in detail" or "list every object".

Text → image: diffusion

A text encoder turns the prompt into vectors, and a diffusion model starts from random noise and removes it step by step, using cross-attention to the prompt vectors to decide what to draw. Stable Diffusion is the standard open example; SD-Turbo is a version distilled to produce an image in a single step, fast enough for a free Colab GPU. The image deep dive in this module covers diffusion in detail.

Audio → text: speech recognition

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) is captioning for sound. Whisper converts audio to a spectrogram, encodes it, and decodes the transcript token by token. It was trained on 680,000 hours of audio paired with transcripts, so it handles accents and background noise well, but it can still mishear names, numbers, and words that sound alike.

Speech recognition only works for speech. To describe non-speech audio, such as a dog barking or a piano piece, you need an audio captioning model, or a shared audio–text space like CLAP that scores how well candidate descriptions match the sound.

Text → audio: speech and music

Text-to-speech (TTS) models predict an acoustic representation of the text, usually a spectrogram, and a vocoder turns that into a waveform. Open examples include Meta's MMS-TTS, which supports over 1,100 languages, and Suno's Bark. To generate music or sound effects instead of speech, models such as Meta's MusicGen predict audio codec tokens conditioned on the text, which the audio deep dive explains.

Image ↔ audio: usually through text

Few models convert directly between images and sound, because there is little paired training data: the internet has billions of captioned images but few images paired with matching audio. In practice, systems go image → text → audio. A shared embedding space such as ImageBind can still retrieve a matching sound for an image, even though it cannot generate one.

3. Compressing and Inventing

The directions on the map are not equally hard, and they fail in different ways. The key question is how much information each side holds.

Direction typeExampleWhat happens to informationTypical failure
Many-to-one (understanding)Image → captionCompressed. Many different images fit the same caption, so details are thrown away.Leaves out colors, counts, positions, or text in the image
One-to-many (generation)Caption → imageInvented. A caption fits many images, so the model must make up every detail the text did not mention.Adds details that were never there; wrong counts or spelling
Nearly one-to-oneText → speech → textMostly preserved. Spoken words map back to written words.Mishears names, numbers, homophones; loses punctuation and capitalization

A short caption of a photo is a huge compression. A 512 × 512 color image holds 786,432 numbers, while a ten-word caption is about a dozen tokens. When a text-to-image model expands that caption back into a picture, it fills the gap with whatever is most typical in its training data. Ask for "a dog on a beach" and you will get a plausible dog on a plausible beach, but probably not the breed, pose, or weather from the original photo.

Every conversion is a prediction. A model never recovers "the" answer; it picks one likely output. Understanding directions pick the most likely summary, and generation directions pick one likely example. Both are guesses shaped by training data.

Check your understanding: which way loses more?

Question: You caption a photo of three red apples on a wooden table and get "a bowl of fruit". Then you generate an image from that caption. Name two details likely to change, and say which step caused each change.

Answer: The count ("three") and color ("red") were dropped by the captioning step, which compresses. A bowl, other fruit, or a different table were invented by the generation step, which must fill in everything the caption left out.

Key Takeaways

  • Image, text, and audio give six conversion directions. Text is the hub; direct image–audio conversion is rare.
  • Captioning and speech recognition use an encoder for the input modality and a text decoder. Text-to-image and text-to-audio use a generator conditioned on text embeddings.
  • Understanding directions compress and drop details. Generation directions invent details the input did not specify.
  • Every conversion is a prediction, not a lossless translation. The next lesson asks what happens when you chain several of them.
