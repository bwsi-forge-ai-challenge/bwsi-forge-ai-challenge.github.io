Crossing Between Modalities

In the last lesson, we saw how models turn text, images, and audio into numerical representations that neural networks can process. But what happens when we want to go a step further and convert one type of information into another?

Think about the different things an AI system can do. It can describe a photograph, turn a written prompt into an image, transcribe a voice recording, or generate music from a sentence. These tasks all involve moving between modalities.

In this lesson, we'll look at how these conversions work, which models are used for each task, and what can go wrong along the way. One important idea will come up repeatedly: when information moves from one format to another, some details may be lost, while others may need to be filled in by the model.

1. The Conversion Map

Let's focus on three modalities: images, text, and audio.

If we want to convert between any two of them, there are six possible directions. For example, an image can become text, and text can become an image. These sound like similar tasks, but they require different approaches.

Text is a common link between the three. We can describe an image in words, then use those words to generate audio. We can also transcribe speech into text and use the transcript to generate an image.

Directly converting images into audio, or audio into images, is less common. These tasks are possible, but many practical systems use text as an intermediate step.

The key distinction is between understanding an input and generating a new output. A model that describes a picture needs to identify useful information in that picture. A model that creates a picture from text has to decide what the picture should look like.

Let's look at how each direction works.

2. How Each Direction Works

Most of these systems build on ideas we've already encountered: encoders, embeddings, transformers, and generative models. The main difference is what goes into the model and what comes out.

Image → Text: Describing a picture

Imagine uploading a photograph and asking an AI model to describe it.

The model first uses a vision encoder to turn the image into a sequence of vectors representing its patches. A text decoder then uses those representations to generate a description, one token at a time.

This is similar to the encoder-decoder transformer we studied in the Foundations module, except the encoder processes an image rather than text.

A model such as BLIP can generate a short caption. Larger vision-language models, or VLMs, such as SmolVLM and Qwen2-VL, can also follow instructions. Instead of simply asking for a caption, you might ask the model to describe the scene in detail or list the objects it can identify.

Of course, a description is not the same as the original image. The model must decide which details matter, and it may overlook something important.

Text → Image: Creating a picture

Now consider the reverse task. You type a prompt such as "a golden retriever running along a beach at sunset," and the model generates an image.

One popular approach uses a diffusion model. The model starts with random noise and gradually turns it into an image. Throughout this process, information from the text prompt helps guide what the model generates.

A text encoder first converts the prompt into vectors. The diffusion model uses these representations to help determine which visual patterns to produce as it removes noise step by step.

Stable Diffusion is a well-known example of an open text-to-image model. Variants such as SD-Turbo are designed to generate images in fewer steps, making image generation faster.

We'll explore how diffusion models work in more detail in the image deep dive later in this module.

The important thing to remember is that the model isn't retrieving a photograph that matches your prompt. It's generating a new image based on patterns learned during training.

Audio → Text: Recognizing speech

When you ask a model to transcribe a voice recording, you're using a task called automatic speech recognition (ASR).

OpenAI's Whisper is a widely used speech recognition model. It converts audio into a representation that its encoder can process, then generates the transcript token by token.

Whisper was trained on approximately 680,000 hours of audio paired with transcripts. This training helps it handle a range of accents and recording conditions, including some background noise.

However, speech recognition isn't perfect. A recording might contain an unfamiliar name, a number, or two words that sound alike. If the audio is unclear, the model may produce the wrong transcript.

There's also an important distinction between recognizing speech and describing sound.

If you record someone saying, "The train is arriving," speech recognition aims to recover those words. If you record a dog barking or a piano playing, there may be no words to transcribe.

For these cases, you can use an audio captioning model to describe the sound, or use a model such as CLAP to compare the recording with possible descriptions.

For example, CLAP can help compare an audio recording against descriptions such as "a dog barking" and "a car engine starting." It produces similarity scores rather than a written transcript.

Text → Audio: Generating speech and music

Converting text into audio can mean several different things.

Text-to-speech (TTS) models turn written words into spoken audio. They typically predict an intermediate representation of the sound, such as a spectrogram, and use a vocoder to convert that representation into an audio waveform.

Examples include Meta's MMS-TTS, which supports many languages, and Bark, which can generate speech and other audio.

Generating music is a different task. A model needs to produce musical patterns, rhythms, and sounds rather than simply pronounce words.

MusicGen, developed by Meta, generates music based on a text description. It predicts audio codec tokens conditioned on the prompt. These tokens represent compressed pieces of audio that can be decoded into a sound recording.

For example, a prompt such as "slow instrumental music with soft piano and strings" gives the model a description to work from, but the actual melody and arrangement are generated by the model.

We'll explore audio representations and generation in more detail in the audio deep dive.

Image ↔ Audio: Why text often comes in between

Suppose you want to generate the sound of a scene shown in a photograph. You might imagine a direct image-to-audio model that looks at the scene and produces an appropriate recording.

In practice, this kind of direct conversion is less common than image-to-text or text-to-audio systems. One reason is that training requires suitable examples connecting images with the sounds they represent. Although the internet contains enormous numbers of images with captions, matching images and audio recordings are less readily available for many tasks.

A practical alternative is to use two models in sequence:

  1. An image captioning model describes the scene.
  2. An audio generation model uses that description to create sound.

For example, an image of a busy street might be described as "cars driving past pedestrians on a city road." An audio model could then generate a plausible street ambience from that description.

But notice the limitation: the generated audio is based on the description, not on a recording of the actual scene. The model doesn't know exactly which sounds were present in the original photograph.

The reverse direction can work similarly. A model describes or transcribes an audio recording, and a text-to-image model uses that description to create a picture.

There are also models such as ImageBind that can connect representations from different modalities. They can help retrieve sounds related to an image, for example, without necessarily generating a new audio recording.

3. Compressing and Inventing

Not all conversions preserve information equally well. To understand why, let's look at what happens when information passes from one modality to another.

Consider a photograph of three red apples on a wooden table.

If a model describes the image as "a bowl of fruit," the description captures the general idea, but it leaves out several details. We no longer know that there were exactly three apples, that they were red, or that they were on a wooden table.

Now suppose we give the caption "a bowl of fruit" to an image generation model. It might create a picture with three apples, but it could just as easily generate five apples, add oranges, or place the bowl on a different table.

The first model discarded details. The second model had to choose details that the prompt didn't specify.

This gives us three useful ways to think about conversions:

Type of conversionExampleWhat happens to the information?Common mistakes
Many-to-one: understandingImage → captionThe input is compressed into a shorter description. Some details are left out.Missing colors, counts, positions, or text

Why descriptions lose so much information

A 512 × 512 color image contains 786,432 pixel values. A short, ten-word caption may contain only around a dozen text tokens.

These two representations contain very different amounts of information. A caption can tell us what's happening in an image, but it usually can't preserve every visual detail.

This isn't necessarily a problem. If you ask for a photograph to be summarized, you probably don't need every pixel described individually. The problem arises when a later task depends on a detail that the caption left out.

The same issue appears during generation. A prompt such as "a dog on a beach" doesn't specify the dog's breed, the time of day, the weather, or the exact position of the dog. The model has to make choices about all of these things.

It draws on patterns learned during training to produce an image that fits the prompt. The result may look convincing, but it isn't guaranteed to match any particular real-world scene.

A conversion is a prediction, not a perfect copy of the original information. When a model summarizes something, it predicts which details belong in the summary. When it generates something, it predicts what a plausible output should look like or sound like.

This is why the direction of a conversion matters. Understanding tasks often leave information out, while generation tasks often fill in missing information.

The first step loses information; the second fills in what is missing.

4. Choosing a Model for Each Link

There are many models for each conversion task. Some are smaller and faster, while others can handle more complicated inputs or produce more detailed outputs.

This is the same tradeoff we discussed in the Training & Tradeoffs unit: a larger model may offer better results, but it can also require more memory and computation.

The best choice depends on what you're trying to do. If you only need a short image caption, a small model may be enough. If you want detailed answers to questions about a complex image, you may want to try a vision-language model instead.

For Project 1, the goal isn't simply to find the most powerful model. It's to understand what each model does and what happens when you connect several models together.

Key Takeaways

  • There are six conversion directions between images, text, and audio. Text is often used as a bridge when connecting images and sound.
  • Different tasks need different models. Image captioning and speech recognition turn inputs into text, while text-to-image and text-to-audio models generate new content.
  • Understanding and generation have different limitations. Understanding models may leave out details, while generative models must fill in details that weren't specified.
  • Conversions are not perfect translations. Each model makes predictions based on its input and what it learned during training.
  • Chaining models can compound these problems. If one model drops a detail, a later model may have no way to recover it. In the next lesson, we'll explore what happens when we chain several models together.
